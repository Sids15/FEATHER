\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Accuracy on launch day is not accuracy forever. Once a model is in
production the data it sees drifts: users behave differently, a sensor
gets swapped out, the lighting changes, the vocabulary moves on, fraud
adapts to whatever caught it last time. Some of that the model absorbs
without trouble. Some of it slowly breaks the model while nobody is
watching. None of this is news. Sculley et al.\ \cite{sculley2015debt}
already flagged changing input distributions as one of the main sources
of technical debt in production machine learning systems, and that was
back in 2015. Today the topic has a name and a literature of its own:
\emph{concept drift} \cite{gama2014survey, lu2018learning}.

What the drift costs depends on where the model sits. A recommender that
slowly goes stale loses some engagement. A credit-risk model loses
money, and maybe draws a regulator's eye. A triage model in a hospital,
or the perception stack in a car, can cost a great deal more than that.
The dangerous thing, though, is not really the size of the failure but
how quiet it is. A model whose assumptions have expired does not throw
an error. It keeps handing back predictions that look perfectly
ordinary, and it goes on being wrong, at scale, for as long as it takes
somebody to notice.

Spotting change, on its own, is not the difficult part. Sequential
change detectors have been around since Page's CUSUM chart in 1954
\cite{page1954cusum}, and for the last twenty years or so the
stream-mining community has been adapting them to learning systems
\cite{gama2004learning, baena2006eddm, bifet2007adwin, raab2020kswin}.
The difficulty is that ``did the data change?'' is the wrong question.
In any real stream the data is always changing. What the person running
the model actually wants to know is whether the model still works, and
the labels that would answer that show up late, or only in dribs and
drabs, or never. A loan takes months to default. A support ticket that
got routed to the wrong queue is quietly fixed by whoever it lands on,
who has no channel back to the model. Because those labels are missing,
every monitor you can actually deploy is standing in for accuracy rather
than measuring it. Our claim is that the field has not looked hard
enough at which stand-in it picks.

This paper is the introductory piece of an ongoing project we call
FEATHER (Fisher-Eigenvalue Adaptive Thresholding for Error-Robust drift
detection). We have kept its scope narrow on purpose. How the method is
actually built, the theory behind it, and the results on deep networks
all belong to the full manuscript we are still writing, not to this
paper. What we do here is lay the ground the project rests on.
Section~\ref{sec:background} says what concept drift is and why, in
operation, the split that matters is harmful versus benign rather than
the textbook taxonomy. Section~\ref{sec:survey} goes through the
existing monitor families and the gap they leave open.
Section~\ref{sec:problem} pins down the problem and what we would demand
of any decent solution. Section~\ref{sec:project} is the methodology:
the guiding hypothesis, the evaluation plan, and the feasibility case.
Section~\ref{sec:synthetic} gives early results on a synthetic testbed,
and Sect.~\ref{sec:outcomes} looks at what we expect to come out of the
project and what lies past it.

\section{Background: What Concept Drift Is}
\label{sec:background}

Write $X$ for the model's input and $Y$ for the target. A supervised
model learns from samples of a joint distribution $P(X, Y)$, and
\emph{dataset shift} is the umbrella term for any change in that
distribution between training time and serving time
\cite{quinonero2009dataset}. It usually gets split into three kinds. In
\emph{covariate shift} the input marginal $P(X)$ moves but the concept
$P(Y \mid X)$ stays put --- a new camera sensor, say, or a different mix
of users. In \emph{label shift} the class balance $P(Y)$ changes while
the classes themselves look the way they always did; a flu season means
more flu patients, not a different-looking flu \cite{lipton2018label}.
In \emph{concept shift} the mapping $P(Y \mid X)$ itself moves, so the
transaction that was legitimate last year reads as fraud this year. Real
streams, of course, do all three at once. And there is the separate
question of timing, whether the change is abrupt, gradual, incremental,
or comes and goes in cycles \cite{gama2014survey, lu2018learning}, so a
detector tuned to sudden jumps can sleep right through a slow drift.

All of that classifies the data. It does not answer the question an
operator actually has, which is simpler and harder: did the shift hurt
the model? The two questions come apart more often than you would think.
A big, obvious covariate shift can leave a robust model completely
unbothered, while a small, statistically unremarkable shift that happens
to land on a sensitive stretch of the decision boundary can be a
disaster. So, following recent usage \cite{amoukou2024sequential}, we
call a shift \emph{harmful} if it measurably drops the deployed model's
accuracy and \emph{benign} if it does not, and we organize the whole
project around that split instead of the covariate/label/concept one.
There is evidence this is the right call: Rabanser et al.\
\cite{rabanser2019failing} found that how loudly a shift is detected has
little to do with how much damage it does. A monitor that reports
``something changed'' and stops there has handed the actual work to
whoever is on call.

Retraining is not a way out of this, either. It wants the very labels
the stream is not giving you, it does not tell anyone that something
went wrong, and it does nothing about the predictions already served
before the new model went in. Monitoring and retraining sit alongside
each other; one does not replace the other \cite{lu2018learning}.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:survey}

The existing work sorts fairly cleanly into three families, by which
signal each one watches: the labels (when you have them), the inputs, or
the model's own outputs. Table~\ref{tab:families} lays the three side by
side.

\subsection{Error-Rate Detectors: The Right Question, the Wrong Input}

The classical stream-mining detectors go straight at the model's error
rate, an idea lifted from statistical process control
\cite{page1954cusum}. DDM fires once the online error rate climbs a set
margin above its running minimum \cite{gama2004learning}; EDDM reworks
the same idea for drift that comes on gradually \cite{baena2006eddm};
ADWIN holds an adaptive window and chops it whenever two halves of that
window start to disagree, and it comes with real false-positive
guarantees \cite{bifet2007adwin}. These are all asking the right thing,
is the model failing, but to do it they need the one thing deployment
refuses to hand over, which is a label for every single prediction. When
the labels are late, all this really buys you is that silent failure
turns into slow-motion failure, and slow-motion failure is not the same
as catching the problem in time. The River library \cite{montiel2021river}
ships solid implementations, which at least makes them easy baselines to
run against.

\subsection{Input-Distribution Tests: Sensitive but Indiscriminate}

The second family gives up on labels and asks instead whether the
incoming inputs still resemble the reference data. The machinery is
two-sample testing: kernel statistics like Maximum Mean Discrepancy
\cite{gretton2012kernel}, windowed Kolmogorov--Smirnov tests in the
style of KSWIN \cite{raab2020kswin}, and lower-dimensional versions that
run the test on the model's representations rather than on raw pixels
\cite{rabanser2019failing}. Where the shift has structure, as label
shift does, there are purpose-built estimators \cite{lipton2018label}.

Being sensitive is exactly what makes this family useful, and it is also
what breaks it. The statistic is measuring how far apart two
distributions are, not what the gap does to the model. Rabanser et al.\
\cite{rabanser2019failing} showed these detectors go off dependably on
synthetic corruptions, the harmless ones very much included. Page
someone for every seasonal wobble and they stop reading the pages, and
an alarm nobody reads is protecting nothing. That same paper lists
characterizing harmful shift as an open problem, and that open problem
is the one we are going after.

\subsection{Output-Based Estimators: The Right Question, a Compromised Witness}

The newest family reads the model's health off its own outputs. The
starting point was the maximum-softmax-probability baseline, which
showed a classifier's confidence does carry real signal about whether it
is right \cite{hendrycks2017baseline}. From there people turned
confidence into actual accuracy estimates: through confidence
differences \cite{guillory2021doc}, through a learned confidence
threshold whose exceedance rate tracks accuracy \cite{garg2022atc}, and
through a prediction-uncertainty index that tends to move before the
error rate does \cite{pudd2025}. The nearest thing to our own framing is
Amoukou et al.\ \cite{amoukou2024sequential}, who set up sequential
harmful-shift detection without labels: an error-proxy scores the stream
and a sequential test holds the false-alarm rate in check. Meanwhile the
statistics side has carried process-control charts over to model
monitoring by way of score vectors \cite{zhang2023score}.

This is the first family that actually asks the operator's question, and
a lot of the time it answers it well. The problem is the witness it
relies on. Deep networks are miscalibrated in a fairly systematic way
even on in-distribution data \cite{guo2017calibration}, and the
calibration only gets worse under precisely the distribution shift the
monitor exists to catch \cite{ovadia2019trust}. Building on a sensor
that is at its worst exactly when you need it most is an uneasy place to
stand.

There is a second problem, separate from calibration, and we do not
think it has been looked at closely. Any statistic you compute from a
model's outputs is, by its very construction, blind to whatever those
outputs do not encode. Pinning harm to accuracy does not get you out of
this, because accuracy is a function of both the predictions and the
true labels. Picture a stream that shifts in a way that leaves every
prediction frozen while the correct answers underneath it quietly move:
a monitor that only watches the outputs sees nothing, and it sees
nothing in exactly the case where the failure is silent. So is this
region of ``inexpressible'' change just a small technicality, or is it
large and structured? That turns out to be a sharp, answerable question
about the architecture itself. It is where the project begins, and
everything more we have to say about it we are holding for the full
manuscript.

\subsection{Feature-Space Methods and Benchmarks}

A related line comes from out-of-distribution detection, which does its
work in the model's penultimate feature space. The Mahalanobis method
scores each sample against class-conditional Gaussians fitted in that
space \cite{lee2018mahalanobis}, and representation-level two-sample
tests came out as the strongest detectors in Rabanser et al.\
\cite{rabanser2019failing}. Feature space is a sensible place to look;
it is where the model has already boiled the input down to what it
thinks matters. The catch is that these methods rank directions by
variance or density, not by how much the task depends on them, so they
end up just as performance-agnostic as the raw input tests.

For evaluation, most of the field leans on corruption suites, CIFAR-10-C
above all, with its 19 corruption types at 5 severities each
\cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking}. Rotated-digit streams show up at
smaller scale, and WILDS supplies shift that occurs naturally rather
than by design \cite{koh2021wilds}. One thing about these benchmarks
matters later on: they grade how detectable a corruption is, not how
much it costs. The name on the corruption tells you nothing about
whether this particular model actually loses accuracy to it. That gap is
the reason we measure harmfulness directly instead of taking it for
granted.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}

With that background, here is the problem we set ourselves. A classifier
$f$ is deployed and frozen. Batches of unlabeled data come in as a
stream. Once, offline, before any of this starts, we get a single
labeled held-out set drawn from the training-time distribution. From
then on the monitor has to make a call batch by batch: has the stream
drifted in a way that would plausibly cost $f$ some accuracy? And, just
as importantly, it has to keep quiet when the drift is something $f$ can
handle.

One thing we want to be firm about from the start is how ground truth
gets decided. In every experiment, whether an episode is harmful is
\emph{measured}, by scoring the deployed model on held-out labels for
that episode. We never read it off the name of the perturbation or how
severe it is supposed to be. A shift that looks dramatic but costs no
accuracy is benign as far as we are concerned; a quiet one that costs a
lot is harmful. That is what keeps the evaluation clear of the benchmark
problem we pointed out in Sect.~\ref{sec:survey}.

Out of all this, four requirements fall out for a monitor worth having:

\begin{enumerate}
\item{\textbf{Label-free online.} Once the one-off offline phase is done,
  the monitor only ever sees unlabeled batches.}
\item{\textbf{Harm-oriented.} An alarm has to track the model's expected
  performance, not distance between distributions for its own sake.}
\item{\textbf{Calibrated.} The operator picks the steady-state
  false-alarm rate ahead of time; it should not be an accident of some
  hand-tuned constant. Fire too often and the monitor gets switched off.}
\item{\textbf{Cheap.} Monitoring has to cost only a small slice of the
  inference it is watching, or the team running things will turn it off
  the first time load spikes.}
\end{enumerate}

Nothing in Table~\ref{tab:families} clears all four. Error-rate charts
fall down on~(1). Input tests fall down on~(2). Output-based estimators
manage both~(1) and~(2), but they carry the reliability problems on
record in \cite{guo2017calibration, ovadia2019trust}, on top of the
structural blind spot we raised a moment ago.

\section{Proposed Methodology}
\label{sec:project}

\subsection{Guiding Hypothesis}

FEATHER is our try at building the monitor around the deployed model's
own geometry instead of around its raw data or its final outputs. The
intuition behind it is not complicated. A trained classifier does not
lean on its internal representation evenly: some directions in feature
space carry the information its decisions actually turn on, and others
provably carry none. Now suppose you could nail down that split for the
one specific model in front of you, rather than on average across
models. Then you could sort drift by \emph{where it lands} relative to
what the model is sensitive to. That sorting is the harm-oriented test
of requirement~(2), the one that neither the input tests nor the output
statistics can give you.

The object we lean on is the model's Fisher information, a classical
piece of information geometry \cite{amari1998natural}. We use it in the
empirical, label-conditioned form, which is what a single offline
reference set actually lets you compute. The gap between this empirical
version and the true Fisher is well understood
\cite{kunstner2019empirical}, and it does not touch the structural
property we depend on. Fisher information is hardly exotic here: it is
central to optimization \cite{martens2015kfac}, it shows up in the
analysis of deep networks, whose Fisher spectra pile up in a handful of
directions \cite{karakida2019universal}, and it drives continual
learning, where it decides which weights are too important to disturb
\cite{kirkpatrick2017overcoming}. The reason it fits monitoring is the
same reason it fits those settings: it measures how sensitive the
model's predictions are to a change, and sensitivity is the whole point
of the harmful-versus-benign split. Geometry has started showing up in
monitoring, too, from score-vector control charts \cite{zhang2023score}
to null-direction analyses of language-model representations
\cite{pandey2025zdp}, so the timing is good. There are also label-free
detectors that read network activations directly, with no task
supervision at all \cite{komorniczak2024parallel}. Still, as far as we
can tell, nobody has tied this geometry to the harmful-versus-benign
call that operators actually need, and done it inside the calibration
and cost limits of requirements~(3) and~(4).

We are keeping the construction at this level of detail deliberately.
The exact formulation, the guarantees we can prove for it, and the
experimental findings all belong to the full manuscript in preparation.
The job of this paper is narrower: set out the problem, the gap, and the
plan.

\subsection{A First Look, in Pictures}
\label{sec:glimpse}

Figure~\ref{fig:geometry-toy} is the smallest picture that still carries
the point. Take a frozen two-class classifier living in a
two-dimensional feature space, with the dashed line as its decision
boundary, and let the data drift. Push it along the red arrow and the
classes slide across the boundary: predictions flip, confidence and
entropy move, and every output-based monitor from
Sect.~\ref{sec:survey} has something to grab onto. Push it the same
distance along the violet arrow, parallel to the boundary this time, and
nothing happens to the outputs. Not a single prediction changes.
Confidence and entropy read exactly as they did before. No matter how
far the data slides along that direction, every output statistic reports
a clean bill of health.

Neither arrow is drawn by hand. Both come straight out of the frozen
model, from the Fisher-information analysis the project is built on. In
two dimensions this all looks like a party trick. Whether it means
anything in practice hangs on two things: how big the invisible region
actually is for real architectures, and whether the drift that does
damage really lands inside it. The first has an exact answer, and a
surprising one, we think; RQ1 states it and the full manuscript proves
it. The second is what the benchmark study is there to measure. We are
keeping both answers out of this paper on purpose. The figure is meant
to show \emph{what} we take advantage of, not \emph{how} it is computed
for a real network, which is the technical core we are holding back.

\subsection{Research Questions}

Three questions run through the whole project:

\begin{enumerate}
\item{\textbf{RQ1 (characterization).} For a standard classifier
  architecture, what can and cannot be picked up by any monitor built
  from the model's outputs on the incoming data? And can that boundary
  be written down exactly, not just estimated empirically?}
\item{\textbf{RQ2 (mechanism).} Can the Fisher geometry of the deployed
  model be turned into a streaming monitor that is label-free,
  calibrated, and cheap (requirements 1, 3, and 4) without giving up on
  being harm-oriented (requirement 2)?}
\item{\textbf{RQ3 (evidence).} On benchmarks where harmfulness is
  measured rather than assumed, does the geometric monitor actually
  track the measured damage, and what does it buy over output-based
  baselines and over stripped-down, geometry-free versions of itself?}
\end{enumerate}

In concrete terms, the project will hand back four things: a formal
answer to RQ1 for softmax classifiers; a monitor for RQ2 whose per-batch
cost is one small linear-algebra step; a calibration routine that turns
the false-alarm budget into a knob the operator sets; and the RQ3
evaluation itself. What we expect to be new is the join. Earlier work
has the harmful-shift problem but not the geometry
\cite{amoukou2024sequential}, or the geometric machinery but not the
harmful-versus-benign question \cite{zhang2023score, pandey2025zdp}.
FEATHER sits at the point where the two meet.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\runinhead{Datasets and Streams.} Two benchmarks to start. Rotated
MNIST, where we spin the test set in fixed angular steps to get a clean
dial for incremental drift, and CIFAR-10-C
\cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking}, with its 19 corruption types at 5
severities plus the clean set. Each is streamed in batches at a model
trained on the clean data. We run several training seeds all the way
through, so every number we report comes with a spread and not just a
single point. If the schedule allows, we would like to add one naturally
occurring shift from WILDS \cite{koh2021wilds} as a stretch goal;
Camelyon17 is the likely candidate, since it is not too large and its
harm is graded rather than synthetically manufactured.

\runinhead{Harm Labeling.} Keeping the promise from
Sect.~\ref{sec:problem}, each episode is tagged harmful or benign by the
accuracy it actually loses relative to the clean episode. Episodes that
fall in the gray band between the two thresholds are dropped from
detection scoring rather than quietly shoved onto one side or the other.

\runinhead{Baselines.} We line up one representative from each family in
Table~\ref{tab:families}. The classical stream detectors, run through
River \cite{montiel2021river}, go in wherever labels are available.
Confidence- and entropy-based output statistics \cite{hendrycks2017baseline,
garg2022atc} are the label-free comparison. An error-proxy sequential
detector, built in the spirit of Amoukou et al.\
\cite{amoukou2024sequential} as the nearest prior framing, gets its
proxy fit on the same labeled calibration data our own monitor uses. The
comparison we care about most, though, is against a variance-weighted
feature-space monitor that keeps our whole pipeline intact but swaps
activation covariance in where we use Fisher information, at identical
calibration. That one baseline asks a single clean question: does
weighting directions by the task beat weighting them by plain density,
the way earlier methods did \cite{lee2018mahalanobis}? Calibration is
held-out throughout. We split the reference data deterministically, one
half to fix each monitor's geometry and the other, disjoint, half to set
its thresholds, so no threshold is ever calibrated on the same data that
shaped the statistic it is gating.

\runinhead{Metrics.} Synthetic corruptions have a way of being obvious
to every family of detector at the same time, so a threshold-free split
of harmful from benign episodes (AUROC) can saturate and end up ranking
nothing. We report it anyway, for completeness. The metric we actually
lead with on these benchmarks is \emph{how the monitor behaves on
measured-benign episodes at matched calibration}, its false-alarm
discipline, in other words. The literature tends to underweight it, and
it keeps telling methods apart well after the detection numbers have
saturated. On top of both, we time things: the wall-clock cost of the
offline fit and of the per-batch online step, set against the cost of
inference itself.

\subsection{Tools and Technology}

PyTorch and torchvision handle the models and the data. The monitor's
linear algebra is plain NumPy, on purpose, so that the online step never
has to touch a GPU. River \cite{montiel2021river} covers the classical
baselines, matplotlib the figures, pytest the verification suite. The
whole thing runs on a single workstation with one 24\,GB GPU; none of it
needs cluster-scale compute. The repository is under version control
together with the training logs, and every figure and table in the paper
is regenerated by a committed script.

As the conference guidelines on research tools ask, we note that an
LLM-based coding assistant (Claude, Anthropic) helped scaffold code and
edit prose. The hypothesis, the theory, the experimental design, and the
interpretation are our own. Every number and figure comes out of
committed scripts we checked ourselves, and the test suite of
Sect.~\ref{sec:synthetic} checks the implementation against analytic
ground truth rather than against anything the assistant produced.

\subsection{Feasibility and Constraints}

The project is sized to fit one semester and a team of two. On data,
everything we use is public; there is nothing to collect and nothing to
annotate. On compute, the benchmark models train in minutes on the GPU
we have, and the monitor costs far less than the inference it sits on
top of, so we are talking about an experimental budget of hours, not
weeks. On skills, the theory lives in undergraduate linear algebra and
probability, and the engineering is ordinary PyTorch. On schedule, the
theory and the synthetic verification are already done
(Sect.~\ref{sec:synthetic}). The training and monitoring infrastructure
is built, and the benchmark runs on both suites are finished,
output-baseline comparison and all, at five seeds per model with
held-out calibration throughout. What is left is the WILDS stretch goal
and the writing, and those can be divided between the two of us.

Four risks stand out, and each has a plan behind it. First, the theory
might not carry over from the synthetic setting to real deep networks.
The synthetic gate in Sect.~\ref{sec:synthetic} is there to catch that
early, since every prediction is checked analytically before a single
deep model is trained, and whichever way the transfer goes, we will
report it straight. Second, the benchmarks might saturate, with every
detector doing well. If that happens, the primary metric we committed to
in advance (false-alarm discipline at matched calibration) and the
covariance baseline both keep drawing distinctions, and the WILDS
stretch task brings in a setting that is not synthetic at all. The
finished runs actually bore this out: threshold-free detection did
saturate across the board, and the primary metric still pulled FEATHER
apart from its covariance ablation, and it did so even when FEATHER's
reference was calibrated on the deployment stream, the same way the
output baselines are, so the gap is not just an artifact of handing
FEATHER a privileged reference (the measured numbers are in the full
manuscript). Third, we might run short on compute; small models and
checkpoint-resume everywhere mean an interrupted run costs us minutes
rather than days. Fourth, the writing could get squeezed at the end, but
since the figures and tables are all script-generated, late results drop
into the manuscript without anyone redoing them by hand.

\section{Preliminary Results: Synthetic Validation}
\label{sec:synthetic}

Before touching a deep network, we put the hypothesis on a testbed where
every quantity has a closed-form answer to check against: streams of
two-dimensional, two-Gaussian data run at a frozen classifier whose
ideal boundary we can write down analytically. On that testbed, the
direction our analysis says should be output-invisible lines up with the
analytic one to numerical precision, better than $1 - 10^{-6}$ in
absolute cosine. The two experiments in Fig.~\ref{fig:synthetic-demo}
then take the two arrows of Fig.~\ref{fig:geometry-toy} head-on. Drift
four standard deviations along the predicted invisible direction and the
model's outputs move by less than $10^{-10}$ per sample, so the
invisibility is exact, not a rounding story, and yet the calibrated
monitor alarms on all fifteen post-onset batches. Drift the same
distance along the visible direction and the monitor sits at its noise
floor, which is what we want, because that drift is precisely the kind
the output-based tools already handle. Across two hundred clean batches,
the steady-state false-alarm rate stays inside its calibrated budget.
The suite behind all of this is now 69 tests, covering the theory's
predictions, the monitor's behavior, the held-out calibration protocol,
and the output baselines. It is committed with the code and re-runs in
seconds on a laptop CPU, so every number in this paragraph is there to
be re-checked whenever.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:outcomes}

Past the concrete deliverables in Sect.~\ref{sec:project}, the
characterization, the monitoring library, the benchmark study, and the
full manuscript, there is a softer outcome we care about just as much,
which is being honest about the negative space. Wherever the benchmarks
cannot tell methods apart, we intend to say so plainly and say why. And
we would be glad if two habits outlasted the particular method: measuring
harm rather than assuming it, and grading false-alarm discipline right
next to raw detection power.

Looking past this semester, two directions look worth chasing. One is
the benchmarks. Corruption suites mostly hand you shifts that every
family of monitor can see, so a benchmark aimed at the \emph{silent}
regime, realistic drift that leaves the model's outputs untouched, in
the spirit of the natural shifts collected in WILDS \cite{koh2021wilds},
would give the field a much sharper instrument. The other is scope. The
geometric view ought to reach past the classifier head into deeper
layers, where Kronecker-factored approximations \cite{martens2015kfac}
keep the objects involved tractable, and it ought to reach toward the
representational-drift questions people are now asking of large language
models \cite{pandey2025zdp}. Neither fits inside the current project; we
mark them as the natural next steps.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

As things stand, label-free monitoring makes you pick between alarming
at everything and trusting a witness with a documented history of
unreliability. We have gone through the landscape and made the case that
what is missing from it is the geometry of the deployed model. FEATHER
is our attempt to turn that one observation into three concrete things:
a formal account of what output-based monitoring simply cannot see, a
streaming monitor light enough to run in practice and built on the
model's Fisher information, and an evaluation that takes both the
harmful-versus-benign split and false-alarm discipline seriously. The
construction, the guarantees, and the measured results are for the full
manuscript. The problem, the gap, the plan, and the case that all of it
is feasible are what we have tried to set down here.
