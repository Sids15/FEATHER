\abstract*{Machine learning models degrade after deployment because the data
they receive keeps changing, and the labels that would reveal the damage
arrive late or never. Existing label-free monitors either alarm on every
distribution change, harmful or not, or infer health from the model's own
outputs --- confidence, entropy, uncertainty --- signals that are themselves
unreliable under the very shifts they are meant to detect. This paper
introduces FEATHER (Fisher-Eigenvalue Adaptive Thresholding for

problem from the geometry of the deployed model itself: rather than asking whether the data
changed, it asks whether the change occurred somewhere the model's behavior
can respond to at all. We review the concept-drift problem and its
taxonomy, survey the main families of monitoring methods and the gap
between them, formulate the harmful-drift detection problem we target,
state our research questions, objectives, and evaluation plan on standard
drift benchmarks, analyze feasibility and risks, and outline the work
ahead.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A classifier that was accurate on the day it shipped does not stay accurate
for free. The data it sees in production drifts --- new user behavior, new
devices and sensors, new lighting conditions, new vocabulary, new fraud
patterns --- and the drift is only sometimes harmless. Practitioners have
known this for a long time. Sculley et al.\ counted changing input
distributions and undeclared data dependencies among the chief sources of
technical debt in real machine learning systems a decade ago
\cite{sculley2015debt}, and the phenomenon has since acquired its own
literature under the name \emph{concept drift} \cite{gama2014survey,
lu2018learning}.

The stakes scale with the application. A recommendation model that decays
costs engagement; a credit-risk model that decays costs money and invites
regulatory trouble; a medical triage or driver-assistance model that decays
can cost considerably more. What makes these failures dangerous is not
their size but their silence. A deployed model does not crash when its
assumptions expire. It keeps returning predictions, formatted exactly like
correct ones, and the pipeline around it keeps consuming them. Between the
moment the world changes and the moment somebody notices, the model is
confidently wrong at scale.

What makes the problem stubborn is not detecting change as such. Statistics
has offered sequential change detectors since Page's 1954 CUSUM chart
\cite{page1954cusum}, and the stream-mining community has spent two decades
refining them for learning systems \cite{gama2004learning, baena2006eddm,
bifet2007adwin, raab2020kswin}. The stubborn part is a mismatch of
questions. The operator of a deployed model does not actually want to know
\emph{did the data change?} --- in any real stream the honest answer is
``constantly.'' The question that matters is \emph{is the model still
working?}, and the labels that would answer it directly arrive late,
sparsely, or never: a loan default takes months to observe, a mislabeled
medical image may never be re-read, a misrouted support ticket is quietly
corrected downstream by a human who has no channel back to the model.
Every practical monitor is therefore a proxy for accuracy, and the choice
of proxy is where current practice, in our view, has an unexamined
weakness.

This paper is the introductory article of an ongoing capstone research
project, FEATHER (Fisher-Eigenvalue Adaptive Thresholding for Error-Robust
drift detection). Its purpose is deliberately limited. We do not present
the method's construction, theory, or experimental results here; those are
the subject of the full manuscript in preparation. What this paper does is
establish the ground the project stands on: Sect.~\ref{sec:background}
reviews what concept drift is and why the harmful/benign distinction is
the operationally important one; Sect.~\ref{sec:survey} surveys the main
families of label-free monitoring methods and locates the gap between
them; Sect.~\ref{sec:problem} formulates the problem we target and the
requirements a satisfying solution must meet; Sect.~\ref{sec:project}
introduces the FEATHER proposal at the level of its guiding hypothesis and
research questions; Sect.~\ref{sec:plan} lays out the planned methodology,
evaluation protocol, tools, feasibility analysis, and risk assessment; and
Sects.~\ref{sec:outcomes}--\ref{sec:future} state the expected outcomes
and the directions we see beyond the current project.

\section{Background: What Concept Drift Is}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Kinds of Shift}

Let $X$ denote the model's input and $Y$ the target. A supervised model is
trained on samples from a joint distribution $P(X, Y)$, and \emph{dataset
shift} is any change in that joint distribution between training time and
serving time \cite{quinonero2009dataset}. Three canonical decompositions
organize the literature. Under \emph{covariate shift}, the input marginal
$P(X)$ changes while the concept $P(Y \mid X)$ stays fixed: the camera gets
a new sensor, the user base shifts demographics, but the mapping from
input to correct answer is untouched. Under \emph{label shift}, the class
proportions $P(Y)$ change while the class-conditional appearance
$P(X \mid Y)$ is stable --- a flu-season surge changes how many patients
have the flu, not what the flu looks like \cite{lipton2018label}. Under
\emph{concept shift} (or ``real'' concept drift), the mapping
$P(Y \mid X)$ itself changes: the same transaction pattern that was
legitimate last year is fraudulent this year. Real streams mix all three.

\subsection{Temporal Patterns}

Orthogonal to \emph{what} changes is \emph{how} it changes over time.
Following the standard taxonomy \cite{gama2014survey, lu2018learning},
drift can be \emph{sudden} (the distribution switches at a change point),
\emph{gradual} (the stream alternates between old and new concept with
increasing frequency before settling), \emph{incremental} (the
distribution slides continuously from old to new), or \emph{recurring}
(previously seen concepts return, as with seasonality).
Figure~\ref{fig:drift-types} illustrates the four patterns. The pattern
matters to monitor design: a detector tuned for abrupt change points can
be blind to a slow slide, and a detector that adapts too eagerly forgets
concepts that will return.

\subsection{Harmful Versus Benign: The Distinction That Matters}

The taxonomies above classify the \emph{data}. The operator cares about a
classification of \emph{consequences}: did the shift degrade the model?
The two do not line up. A large, easily detectable covariate shift may be
absorbed by a robust model without losing a point of accuracy --- consider
a vision model that was trained with brightness augmentation receiving
brighter images. Conversely, a statistically subtle shift concentrated
exactly where the decision boundary is sensitive can be devastating. We
follow recent usage \cite{amoukou2024sequential} in calling a shift
\emph{harmful} when it materially degrades the deployed model's accuracy
and \emph{benign} when it does not, and we take this distinction ---
not the covariate/label/concept taxonomy --- to be the one a monitoring
system should be built around. This position has empirical backing:
Rabanser et al.\ showed across a large study that the strength of a
detected distribution change correlates poorly with the damage it causes
\cite{rabanser2019failing}, which means a monitor that reports
``change detected'' without qualifying its consequences delegates the hard
part of the problem back to the human reading the alert.

It is worth stating why the obvious alternative --- continuous retraining
--- does not remove the need for monitoring. Retraining requires labels,
which are exactly what the stream lacks; retraining on model-predicted
labels amplifies the model's own errors; and blind periodic retraining
neither tells the operator that an incident occurred nor protects the
predictions served between incident and retrain. Monitoring and adaptation
are complements, not substitutes \cite{lu2018learning}.

\section{A Survey of Label-Free Monitoring}
\label{sec:survey}

We organize existing approaches into three families by the signal they
watch: the labels (when available), the inputs, and the model's outputs.
Table~\ref{tab:families} summarizes the comparison.

\subsection{Error-Rate Detectors: The Right Question, the Wrong Input}

The classical stream-mining detectors monitor the model's error rate
directly and inherit the machinery of statistical process control
\cite{page1954cusum}. DDM tracks the online error rate and alarms when it
exceeds its minimum by a significance margin \cite{gama2004learning}; EDDM
refines the idea for gradual drift by tracking the spacing between errors
rather than their rate \cite{baena2006eddm}; ADWIN maintains an adaptive
window and cuts it when two sub-windows differ significantly, with formal
false-positive guarantees \cite{bifet2007adwin}. These methods ask exactly
the right question --- is the model failing? --- but consume the one
resource the deployment setting withholds: a label for every prediction.
In practice they are used in delayed-label settings (monitor last month's
performance with this month's arrived labels), which converts silent
failure into slow-motion failure but not into timely detection. Mature
implementations of this family ship in the River streaming-learning
library \cite{montiel2021river}, which also makes them convenient
baselines.

\subsection{Input-Distribution Tests: Sensitive but Indiscriminate}

The second family drops the label requirement by testing whether the
arriving inputs still resemble the reference distribution. The tools are
two-sample tests: kernel methods such as Maximum Mean Discrepancy give a
general nonparametric test with strong theory \cite{gretton2012kernel};
windowed Kolmogorov--Smirnov statistics power streaming detectors such as
KSWIN \cite{raab2020kswin}; and dimensionality-reduced variants run the
test on model representations rather than raw pixels
\cite{rabanser2019failing}. For structured shift types there are targeted
estimators, e.g.\ black-box label-shift correction from the confusion
matrix \cite{lipton2018label}.

The strength of this family is sensitivity; its weakness is that it is
\emph{performance-agnostic by design}. The test statistic measures
distributional distance, not consequence. The empirical study of Rabanser
et al.\ \cite{rabanser2019failing} found these detectors fire reliably on
synthetic corruptions --- and equally reliably on harmless ones. An
operator who receives an alarm for every harmless seasonal wobble
habituates, and a monitor whose alarms are habitually ignored protects
nothing. The study explicitly names the characterization of harmful shift
as an open problem, and that is the opening this project aims at.

\subsection{Output-Based Estimators: The Right Question, a Compromised Witness}

The third and most recent family estimates the model's health from the
model's own outputs. The maximum-softmax-probability baseline established
that a classifier's confidence carries usable signal about its
correctness \cite{hendrycks2017baseline}. Its descendants sharpen the
readout into accuracy estimates: Difference of Confidences regresses the
accuracy drop on the confidence drop between distributions
\cite{guillory2021doc}; Averaged Threshold Confidence learns a confidence
threshold on held-out data such that the fraction of stream samples above
it estimates accuracy \cite{garg2022atc}; PUDD builds a
prediction-uncertainty index whose changes flag drift earlier than
error-rate methods \cite{pudd2025}. Closest to our framing, Amoukou et
al.\ formalize \emph{sequential harmful-shift detection without labels}:
an error-proxy model scores the stream and a sequential test controls the
false-alarm rate \cite{amoukou2024sequential}. In parallel, the
statistics community has brought process-control charts to model
monitoring via score vectors \cite{zhang2023score}.

This family asks the operator's question and often answers it well. But
the witness it examines is compromised in two documented ways. First,
modern deep networks are systematically miscalibrated --- their confidence
overstates their accuracy even in distribution \cite{guo2017calibration}.
Second, and worse for monitoring, calibration and uncertainty quality
degrade \emph{further} precisely under the distribution shift the monitor
is supposed to catch, a finding established at scale by Ovadia et al.\
\cite{ovadia2019trust}. A monitor whose sensor becomes least trustworthy
exactly when it is most needed rests on an uncomfortable foundation.

There is also a structural concern, distinct from calibration, that we
believe has not been examined carefully: \emph{any} statistic computed
from a model's outputs is, by construction, unable to notice whatever the
outputs themselves do not express. Whether the region of ``inexpressible''
change is a negligible technicality or large and structured is a precise
mathematical question about the model's architecture --- and it is the
question our project starts from. We defer everything further about it to
the full manuscript.

\subsection{Feature-Space Methods and Benchmarks}

A related line from out-of-distribution detection works in the model's
penultimate feature space rather than on raw inputs or final outputs: the
Mahalanobis method scores samples against class-conditional Gaussians fit
to features \cite{lee2018mahalanobis}, and representation-level two-sample
tests were the strongest detectors in \cite{rabanser2019failing}. Feature
space is the right place to look --- it is where the model summarizes what
it believes about an input --- but existing feature-space methods weight
directions by their variance or density, not by their relevance to the
task, and so remain performance-agnostic in the same way input tests are.

Finally, the benchmarks. Controlled evaluation of drift detectors leans
on corruption suites, above all CIFAR-10-C, which applies 19 corruption
types at 5 severities to a standard test set \cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking};
rotated-digit streams serve the same role at smaller scale. For naturally
occurring shift, WILDS curates in-the-wild distribution shifts across
domains \cite{koh2021wilds}. A limitation we will return to: corruption
benchmarks grade \emph{detectability}, not \emph{consequence} --- the
corruption name and severity say nothing about whether a given model
actually loses accuracy, which is one reason we insist on measuring
harmfulness rather than assuming it.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}

We can now state the problem the project addresses. A frozen classifier
$f$ is deployed. Batches of unlabeled inputs arrive as a stream. A labeled
held-out set from the training-time distribution is available once,
offline, before deployment. The monitoring system must decide, batch by
batch, whether the stream has drifted in a way that plausibly degrades
$f$'s accuracy --- and it must remain quiet under drift that $f$
tolerates.

Ground truth for evaluation deserves care, and we make one methodological
commitment up front: in our experiments, the harmfulness of an episode
will be \emph{measured}, by scoring the deployed model on held-out labels
for that episode, rather than assumed from the name or severity of the
perturbation applied. An episode whose measured accuracy drop is below a
small threshold counts as benign no matter how visually dramatic the
shift; an episode above a large threshold counts as harmful no matter how
subtle. This protects the evaluation from the benchmark limitation noted
in Sect.~\ref{sec:survey}.

From the survey we distill four requirements for a satisfying monitor:

\begin{enumerate}
\item{\textbf{Label-free online.} After the one-time offline phase, the
  monitor sees only unlabeled batches.}
\item{\textbf{Harm-oriented.} The alarm criterion must be connected to the
  deployed model's expected performance, not to distributional distance
  in the abstract.}
\item{\textbf{Calibrated.} The stationary false-alarm rate must be a
  parameter the operator chooses in advance, not an emergent property of
  tuned constants; a monitor that cries wolf gets muted.}
\item{\textbf{Cheap.} Monitoring must cost a small fraction of the
  inference it observes, or production teams will disable it under load.}
\end{enumerate}

No family in Table~\ref{tab:families} meets all four. Error-rate charts
fail (1); input tests fail (2); output-based estimators meet (1) and (2)
but inherit the reliability problems documented in
\cite{guo2017calibration, ovadia2019trust} and the structural concern
raised above.

\section{The FEATHER Proposal}
\label{sec:project}

\subsection{Guiding Hypothesis}

FEATHER is our attempt to build a monitor around the deployed model's own
geometry rather than around its raw data or its final outputs. The guiding
intuition is simple to state. A trained classifier does not use its
internal representation uniformly: some directions in feature space carry
the information its decisions depend on, and others provably do not. If
one can characterize that division --- exactly, for the deployed model at
hand, rather than statistically for models in general --- then drift can
be classified by \emph{where it lands} relative to the model's
sensitivities, which is precisely the harm-oriented criterion requirement
(2) asks for and neither input tests nor output statistics provide.

The mathematical instrument we use for the characterization is the Fisher
information of the model --- a classical object from information geometry
\cite{amari1998natural} that already plays a central role in neural-network
optimization \cite{martens2015kfac}, in the theoretical analysis of deep
networks (where Fisher spectra are known to concentrate in few directions
\cite{karakida2019universal}), and in continual learning, where it decides
which parameters are too important to move \cite{kirkpatrick2017overcoming}.
Its appeal for monitoring is the same as its appeal in those fields: it
measures sensitivity of the model's predictive distribution, which is
exactly the currency the harmful/benign distinction trades in. Geometric
ideas have recently begun to appear in the monitoring literature ---
score-vector control charts \cite{zhang2023score}, null-direction analyses
of language-model representations \cite{pandey2025zdp} --- which we read
as evidence the direction is timely. To our knowledge, however, no
existing method connects this geometry to the harmful-versus-benign
distinction that operators actually need, with the calibration and cost
properties of requirements (3) and (4).

We deliberately keep the construction at this level of description. The
precise formulation, the guarantees we can prove about it, and our
experimental findings are the subject of the full manuscript in
preparation; this paper's role is to establish the problem, the gap, and
the plan.

\subsection{Research Questions}

Three questions structure the work:

\begin{enumerate}
\item{\textbf{RQ1 (characterization).} For standard classifier
  architectures, what exactly can and cannot be detected by any monitor
  computed from the model's outputs? Can the boundary be stated exactly
  rather than empirically?}
\item{\textbf{RQ2 (mechanism).} Can the Fisher geometry of the deployed
  model be turned into a streaming monitor that is label-free, calibrated,
  and cheap --- requirements (1), (3), and (4) --- while remaining
  harm-oriented, requirement (2)?}
\item{\textbf{RQ3 (evidence).} On benchmarks where harmfulness is measured
  rather than assumed, does the geometric monitor track measured damage,
  and what does it add over both output-based baselines and simpler
  geometry-free ablations of itself?}
\end{enumerate}

RQ3's final clause reflects a discipline we want to hold ourselves to: a
new method must be compared not only against prior work but against the
simplest degradation of itself, so that the contribution of each
ingredient is isolated rather than asserted.

\subsection{Objectives and Expected Novelty}

Concretely, the project will deliver: a formal answer to RQ1 for softmax
classifiers; a monitor implementing RQ2, with an offline fitting phase on
the held-out set and an online phase whose per-batch cost is a small
linear-algebra operation; a calibration procedure making the false-alarm
budget an explicit operator-chosen parameter; and the RQ3 evaluation. The
expected novelty is the connection itself: prior work has the
harmful-shift problem without the geometry \cite{amoukou2024sequential} or
geometric machinery without the harmful/benign question
\cite{zhang2023score, pandey2025zdp}; FEATHER is built in the
intersection.

\section{Planned Methodology and Work Plan}
\label{sec:plan}

\subsection{Phases}

The project runs in five phases, designed so that each produces a
verifiable artifact before the next begins.

\runinhead{Phase 1: Theory.} Work out the RQ1 characterization for softmax
classifiers on paper, with proofs at the level of rigor of a methods
paper.

\runinhead{Phase 2: Synthetic Verification.} Implement the monitor and
test it first on low-dimensional synthetic streams whose distributions we
control analytically, so every quantity the theory predicts has a known
ground-truth value to compare against. This phase is complete at the time
of writing: the testbed exists, and the implementation reproduces the
theory's predictions to numerical precision, which we take as the
strongest available evidence of feasibility before the benchmark
experiments run.

\runinhead{Phase 3: Deep-Network Infrastructure.} Train the benchmark
models (a small CNN for the digit experiments, a ResNet-family model for
CIFAR-10) under fixed seeds with full checkpointing, and build the
monitoring harness that streams episodes, logs every statistic, and
regenerates every figure and table from logs by script. Reproducibility
is a first-class requirement: no number enters the paper that a script
cannot recompute.

\runinhead{Phase 4: Benchmark Evaluation.} Execute the protocol of
Sect.~\ref{sec:protocol} on Rotated MNIST and CIFAR-10-C across multiple
seeds, including baselines and ablations.

\runinhead{Phase 5: Writing.} Complete the full manuscript, fold in the
measured results, and prepare the defense material.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\runinhead{Datasets and Streams.} Rotated MNIST (the test set rotated in
fixed angular steps, giving a controllable incremental-drift dial) and
CIFAR-10-C \cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking} (19 corruption types $\times$
5 severities plus the clean set), each streamed in batches against a model
trained on the clean distribution. Multiple training seeds throughout, so
that every reported number carries a spread rather than a point estimate.

\runinhead{Harm Labeling.} As committed in Sect.~\ref{sec:problem},
episodes are labeled harmful or benign by their measured accuracy drop
against the clean episode, with an explicit gray zone between the two
thresholds that is excluded from detection scoring rather than silently
assigned to either side.

\runinhead{Baselines.} Representatives of each family in
Table~\ref{tab:families}: classical stream detectors via River
\cite{montiel2021river} where labels permit, confidence- and entropy-based
output statistics \cite{hendrycks2017baseline, garg2022atc} as the
label-free comparison, and a geometry-free ablation of our own monitor as
the discipline check of RQ3.

\runinhead{Metrics.} Threshold-free separation of harmful from benign
episodes (AUROC); false-alarm behavior on measured-benign episodes at
matched calibration, which the literature tends to underweight relative
to detection power; and wall-clock cost of the offline phase and the
per-batch online step, reported relative to the cost of inference
itself.

\subsection{Tools and Technology}

PyTorch and torchvision for models and data; NumPy for the monitor's
linear algebra (deliberately: the online step should not need a GPU);
River \cite{montiel2021river} for classical baselines; matplotlib for
figures; pytest for the verification suite that gates every phase. All
experiments run on a single workstation with one 24 GB GPU; nothing in
the plan requires cluster-scale compute. The repository is
version-controlled with the training logs, and every artifact in the
papers is regenerated by committed scripts.

\subsection{Feasibility and Constraints}

The project is deliberately scoped to be achievable within one semester by
a two-person team. On \emph{data}: all benchmarks are public and
downloadable; no collection or annotation is needed. On \emph{compute}:
the benchmark models train in minutes on the available GPU, and the
monitoring computations are orders of magnitude cheaper than the
inference they observe, so the experimental budget is hours, not weeks.
On \emph{skills}: the theory sits in undergraduate linear algebra and
probability; the engineering is standard PyTorch. On \emph{schedule}:
Phases 1--2 are complete, Phase 3 is largely built, and the remaining
critical path is benchmark execution and writing, both parallelizable
between the two team members. Table~\ref{tab:risks} lists the main risks
and the mitigations we planned for each.

\section{Expected Outcomes}
\label{sec:outcomes}

The project's deliverables are: (i) the formal characterization answering
RQ1; (ii) an implemented, tested, and documented monitoring library
answering RQ2, usable against any PyTorch classifier exposing its
penultimate features; (iii) the benchmark study answering RQ3, with every
figure and table regenerable from committed scripts and logs; and (iv)
the full manuscript. Two softer outcomes matter to us as well. First, an
honest negative-space report: wherever the standard benchmarks fail to
discriminate between methods, we will say so and explain why, rather than
selecting the comparisons that flatter the proposal. Second, a
methodological argument we hope outlives the specific method: that drift
benchmarks should measure harm rather than assume it, and that new
monitors should be evaluated on their false-alarm discipline, not only
their detection power.

\section{Future Directions}
\label{sec:future}

Beyond the semester, two directions look promising. First, benchmarks.
Current corruption suites mostly produce shifts that every family of
monitor can see, which limits how sharply they can rank methods;
constructing benchmarks for the \emph{silent} regime --- realistic drift
that leaves model outputs untouched, in the spirit of the naturally
occurring shifts cataloged by WILDS \cite{koh2021wilds} rather than
synthetic corruptions --- would give the community a sharper instrument.
Second, scope. The geometric viewpoint should extend beyond the classifier
head toward deeper layers (where Kronecker-factored approximations
\cite{martens2015kfac} make the relevant objects tractable) and toward
other architectures, including the representational-drift questions now
being asked of large language models \cite{pandey2025zdp}. Both
directions are outside the current project's scope, and we flag them as
its natural continuations.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Monitoring a deployed model without labels currently means choosing
between alarms about everything and trust in a witness with documented
reliability problems. We have reviewed the drift-monitoring landscape,
argued that the missing ingredient is the geometry of the deployed model
itself, and introduced FEATHER: a project that develops this observation
into a formal characterization of what output-based monitoring can never
see, a practical streaming monitor built on the Fisher information of the
deployed model, and an evaluation that takes the difference between
harmful and benign drift --- and the discipline of false alarms ---
seriously. The construction, its guarantees, and the measured results are
the subject of the full manuscript; the problem, the gap, the plan, and
the feasibility case are, we hope, made here.
