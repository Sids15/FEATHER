\abstract*{Machine learning models degrade after deployment because the data
they receive keeps changing, and the labels that would reveal the damage
arrive late or never. Existing label-free monitors either alarm on every
distribution change, harmful or not, or infer health from the model's own
outputs --- confidence, entropy, uncertainty --- signals that are themselves
unreliable under the very shifts they are meant to detect. This paper
introduces FEATHER (Fisher-Eigenvalue Adaptive Thresholding for
Error-Robust Drift Detection), an ongoing project that approaches the
problem from the geometry of the deployed model itself: rather than asking whether the data
changed, it asks whether the change occurred somewhere the model's behavior
can respond to at all. We review the concept-drift problem and its
taxonomy, survey the main families of monitoring methods and the gap
between them, formulate the harmful-drift detection problem we target,
state our research questions, objectives, and evaluation plan on standard
drift benchmarks, analyze feasibility and risks, and outline the work
ahead.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A classifier that was accurate the day it shipped does not stay accurate
for free. The data it sees in production drifts --- new user behavior, new
sensors, new lighting, new vocabulary, new fraud patterns --- and the
drift is only sometimes harmless. Practitioners have known this for a long
time: Sculley et al.\ counted changing input distributions among the chief
sources of technical debt in real machine learning systems a decade ago
\cite{sculley2015debt}, and the problem now has its own literature under
the name \emph{concept drift} \cite{gama2014survey, lu2018learning}.

The stakes scale with the application: a recommendation model that decays
costs engagement, a credit-risk model costs money and regulatory trouble,
a medical triage or driver-assistance model can cost far more. What makes
these failures dangerous is not their size but their silence. A deployed
model does not crash when its assumptions expire; it keeps returning
predictions, formatted exactly like correct ones, and between the moment
the world changes and the moment somebody notices, it is confidently
wrong at scale.

What makes the problem stubborn is not detecting change as such ---
statistics has offered sequential change detectors since Page's 1954
CUSUM chart \cite{page1954cusum}, refined for learning systems over two
decades of stream mining \cite{gama2004learning, baena2006eddm,
bifet2007adwin, raab2020kswin}. The stubborn part is a mismatch of
questions. The operator of a deployed model does not actually want to know
\emph{did the data change?} --- in any real stream the honest answer is
``constantly.'' The question that matters is \emph{is the model still
working?}, and the labels that would answer it directly arrive late,
sparsely, or never: a loan default takes months to observe; a misrouted
support ticket is quietly corrected downstream by a human with no channel
back to the model. Every practical monitor is therefore a proxy for
accuracy, and the choice of proxy is where current practice, in our view,
has an unexamined weakness.

This paper is the introductory article of an ongoing research project,
FEATHER (Fisher-Eigenvalue Adaptive Thresholding for Error-Robust drift
detection). Its purpose is deliberately limited: we do not present the
method's construction, theory, or deep-network results, which are the
subject of the full manuscript in preparation. Instead we establish the
ground the project stands on --- what concept drift is and why the
harmful/benign distinction is the operationally important one
(Sect.~\ref{sec:background}); the gap between the existing families of
monitors (Sect.~\ref{sec:survey}); the problem and the requirements a
satisfying solution must meet (Sect.~\ref{sec:problem}); the FEATHER
proposal, with a first look at the idea and the synthetic-testbed
evidence (Sect.~\ref{sec:project}); and the evaluation plan, feasibility,
and outlook (Sects.~\ref{sec:plan}--\ref{sec:future}).

\section{Background: What Concept Drift Is}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Kinds of Shift and Their Timing}

Let $X$ denote the model's input and $Y$ the target. A supervised model is
trained on samples from a joint distribution $P(X, Y)$, and \emph{dataset
shift} is any change in that joint distribution between training and
serving \cite{quinonero2009dataset}. Three decompositions organize the
literature: \emph{covariate shift}, where the input marginal $P(X)$
changes while the concept $P(Y \mid X)$ stays fixed (a new camera sensor,
a shifting user base); \emph{label shift}, where class proportions $P(Y)$
change while class-conditional appearance is stable --- a flu-season surge
changes how many patients have the flu, not what the flu looks like
\cite{lipton2018label}; and \emph{concept shift}, where the mapping
$P(Y \mid X)$ itself changes --- the transaction pattern that was
legitimate last year is fraudulent this year. Real streams mix all three.
Separate from \emph{what} changes is \emph{how}: drift can be sudden,
gradual, incremental, or recurring \cite{gama2014survey, lu2018learning},
and a detector tuned for abrupt change points can be blind to a slow
slide.

\subsection{Harmful Versus Benign: The Distinction That Matters}

The taxonomies above classify the \emph{data}. The operator cares about a
classification of \emph{consequences}: did the shift degrade the model?
The two do not line up. A large, easily detectable covariate shift may be
absorbed by a robust model without losing a point of accuracy, while a
statistically subtle shift concentrated where the decision boundary is
sensitive can be devastating. We follow recent usage
\cite{amoukou2024sequential} in calling a shift \emph{harmful} when it
materially degrades the deployed model's accuracy and \emph{benign} when
it does not, and we take this distinction --- not the
covariate/label/concept taxonomy --- to be the one a monitoring system
should be built around. The position has empirical backing: Rabanser et
al.\ showed that the strength of a detected change correlates poorly with
the damage it causes \cite{rabanser2019failing}, so a monitor that
reports ``change detected'' without qualifying consequences delegates the
hard part back to the human reading the alert.

Continuous retraining does not remove the need for monitoring: it needs
the labels the stream lacks, retraining on model-predicted labels
amplifies the model's own errors, and blind periodic retraining neither
reports the incident nor protects the predictions served before the
retrain. Monitoring and adaptation are complements, not substitutes
\cite{lu2018learning}.

\section{A Survey of Label-Free Monitoring}
\label{sec:survey}

We organize existing approaches into three families by the signal they
watch: the labels (when available), the inputs, and the model's outputs.
Table~\ref{tab:families} summarizes the comparison.

\subsection{Error-Rate Detectors: The Right Question, the Wrong Input}

The classical stream-mining detectors monitor the model's error rate
directly, borrowing from statistical process control
\cite{page1954cusum}: DDM alarms when the online error rate exceeds its
minimum by a significance margin \cite{gama2004learning}, EDDM adapts the
idea to gradual drift \cite{baena2006eddm}, and ADWIN cuts an adaptive
window when two sub-windows differ significantly, with formal
false-positive guarantees \cite{bifet2007adwin}. These methods ask exactly
the right question --- is the model failing? --- but consume the one
resource deployment withholds: a label for every prediction. Used with
delayed labels they convert silent failure into slow-motion failure, not
into timely detection. Mature implementations ship in the River library
\cite{montiel2021river}, which also makes them convenient baselines.

\subsection{Input-Distribution Tests: Sensitive but Indiscriminate}

The second family drops the label requirement by testing whether the
arriving inputs still resemble the reference distribution, using
two-sample tests: kernel statistics such as Maximum Mean Discrepancy
\cite{gretton2012kernel}, windowed Kolmogorov--Smirnov tests as in KSWIN
\cite{raab2020kswin}, and dimensionality-reduced variants that test model
representations rather than raw pixels \cite{rabanser2019failing}, with
targeted estimators for structured cases such as label shift
\cite{lipton2018label}.

The strength of this family is sensitivity; its weakness is that it is
\emph{performance-agnostic by design} --- the statistic measures
distributional distance, not consequence. Rabanser et al.\
\cite{rabanser2019failing} found these detectors fire reliably on
synthetic corruptions and equally reliably on harmless ones. An operator
who gets an alarm for every harmless seasonal wobble soon stops reading
them, and a monitor whose alarms go unread protects nothing. The study
explicitly names the characterization of harmful shift as an open
problem, and that is the opening this project aims at.

\subsection{Output-Based Estimators: The Right Question, a Compromised Witness}

The third and most recent family estimates the model's health from the
model's own outputs. The maximum-softmax-probability baseline established
that a classifier's confidence carries usable signal about its
correctness \cite{hendrycks2017baseline}; its descendants sharpen the
readout into accuracy estimates --- from confidence differences
\cite{guillory2021doc}, from a learned confidence threshold whose
exceedance rate estimates accuracy \cite{garg2022atc}, or from a
prediction-uncertainty index that flags drift earlier than error-rate
methods \cite{pudd2025}. Closest to our framing, Amoukou et al.\
formalize \emph{sequential harmful-shift detection without labels}: an
error-proxy model scores the stream and a sequential test controls the
false-alarm rate \cite{amoukou2024sequential}. In parallel, the
statistics community has brought process-control charts to model
monitoring via score vectors \cite{zhang2023score}.

This family asks the operator's question and often answers it well, but
the witness it examines is compromised in two documented ways: modern
deep networks are systematically miscalibrated even in distribution
\cite{guo2017calibration}, and calibration degrades \emph{further}
precisely under the distribution shift the monitor is supposed to catch
\cite{ovadia2019trust}. A monitor whose sensor is least trustworthy
exactly when it is most needed stands on shaky ground.

There is also a structural concern, distinct from calibration, that we
believe has not been examined carefully: \emph{any} statistic computed
from a model's outputs on the arriving data is, by construction, unable to
notice whatever the outputs themselves do not express. This is not
resolved by defining harm through accuracy, because accuracy depends on
the model's predictions \emph{and} on the ground-truth labels: a stream
can move in ways that leave every prediction frozen while the correct
answers underneath them change, and a monitor blind to such movement is
blind precisely where failure is silent. Whether the region of
``inexpressible'' change is a negligible technicality or large and
structured is a precise mathematical question about the model's
architecture --- and it is the question our project starts from. We defer
everything further about it to the full manuscript.

\subsection{Feature-Space Methods and Benchmarks}

A related line from out-of-distribution detection works in the model's
penultimate feature space: the Mahalanobis method scores samples against
class-conditional Gaussians fit to features \cite{lee2018mahalanobis},
and representation-level two-sample tests were the strongest detectors in
\cite{rabanser2019failing}. Feature space is the right place to look ---
it is where the model summarizes what it believes about an input --- but
these methods weight directions by variance or density, not by relevance
to the task, and so remain performance-agnostic in the same way input
tests are.

Evaluation, finally, leans on corruption suites --- above all CIFAR-10-C,
19 corruption types at 5 severities \cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking} ---
with rotated-digit streams at smaller scale and WILDS for naturally
occurring shift \cite{koh2021wilds}. A limitation we will return to:
corruption benchmarks grade \emph{detectability}, not \emph{consequence}
--- the corruption name says nothing about whether a given model actually
loses accuracy, which is why we insist on measuring harmfulness rather
than assuming it.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}

We can now state the problem the project addresses. A frozen classifier
$f$ is deployed. Batches of unlabeled inputs arrive as a stream. A labeled
held-out set from the training-time distribution is available once,
offline, before deployment. The monitoring system must decide, batch by
batch, whether the stream has drifted in a way that plausibly degrades
$f$'s accuracy --- and it must remain quiet under drift that $f$
tolerates.

One commitment up front about ground truth: in our experiments the
harmfulness of an episode is \emph{measured}, by scoring the deployed
model on held-out labels for that episode, never assumed from the name or
severity of the perturbation. A shift that looks dramatic but costs no
accuracy counts as benign; a subtle one that costs a lot counts as
harmful. This protects the evaluation from the benchmark limitation noted
in Sect.~\ref{sec:survey}.

From the survey we draw four requirements for a satisfying monitor:

\begin{enumerate}
\item{\textbf{Label-free online.} After the one-time offline phase, the
  monitor sees only unlabeled batches.}
\item{\textbf{Harm-oriented.} The alarm must be tied to the deployed
  model's expected performance, not to distribution distance for its own
  sake.}
\item{\textbf{Calibrated.} The stationary false-alarm rate must be
  something the operator chooses in advance, not the by-product of
  hand-tuned constants; a monitor that cries wolf gets muted.}
\item{\textbf{Cheap.} Monitoring must cost a small fraction of the
  inference it observes, or production teams will disable it under load.}
\end{enumerate}

No family in Table~\ref{tab:families} meets all four. Error-rate charts
fail (1); input tests fail (2); output-based estimators meet (1) and (2)
but inherit the reliability problems documented in
\cite{guo2017calibration, ovadia2019trust} and the structural concern
raised above.

\section{The FEATHER Proposal}
\label{sec:project}

\subsection{Guiding Hypothesis}

FEATHER is our attempt to build a monitor around the deployed model's own
geometry rather than around its raw data or its final outputs. The guiding
intuition is simple to state. A trained classifier does not use its
internal representation uniformly: some directions in feature space carry
the information its decisions depend on, and others provably do not. If
one can pin that division down --- exactly, for the deployed model at
hand, rather than statistically for models in general --- then drift can
be classified by \emph{where it lands} relative to what the model is
sensitive to. That is exactly the harm-oriented criterion of requirement
(2), the one neither input tests nor output statistics provide.

The tool we use is the Fisher information of the model --- a classical
object from information geometry \cite{amari1998natural} that already
plays a central role in optimization \cite{martens2015kfac}, in the
analysis of deep networks, whose Fisher spectra concentrate in a few
directions \cite{karakida2019universal}, and in continual learning, where
it decides which parameters are too important to move
\cite{kirkpatrick2017overcoming}. Its appeal for monitoring is the same
as in those fields: it measures how sensitive the model's predictions are
to change, which is exactly what the harmful/benign distinction is about.
Geometric ideas have started to appear in the monitoring literature ---
score-vector control charts \cite{zhang2023score}, null-direction
analyses of language-model representations \cite{pandey2025zdp} --- so
the direction is timely. To our knowledge, though, no existing method
connects this geometry to the harmful-versus-benign distinction operators
actually need, with the calibration and cost of requirements (3)
and~(4).

We deliberately keep the construction at this level of description. The
precise formulation, the guarantees we can prove about it, and our
experimental findings are the subject of the full manuscript in
preparation; this paper's role is to establish the problem, the gap, and
the plan.

\subsection{A First Look, in Pictures}
\label{sec:glimpse}

Figure~\ref{fig:geometry-toy} shows the smallest example that carries the
idea. A frozen two-class classifier lives in a two-dimensional feature
space; the dashed line is its decision boundary. Now let the data drift.
Drift along the red arrow pushes the classes across the boundary:
predictions, confidence, and entropy all react, and every output-based
monitor of Sect.~\ref{sec:survey} has something to work with. Drift along
the violet arrow moves the data just as far --- but parallel to the
boundary. Not one prediction changes. Confidence and entropy read exactly
as before. To every output statistic the world looks healthy, however far
the data travels in that direction.

The two arrows are not drawn by hand; they are computed from the frozen
model by the Fisher-information analysis at the heart of the project. In
two dimensions the split looks like a curiosity. Whether it matters in
practice comes down to two questions: how large the invisible region is
for real architectures, and whether damaging drift actually lands in it.
The first has an exact answer --- and, we think, a surprising one ---
which RQ1 formalizes and the full manuscript proves; the second is what
the benchmark evaluation measures. We keep both answers out of this
introductory paper on purpose: the figure shows \emph{what} we exploit,
not \emph{how} we compute it for a real network, efficiently and exactly,
which is the technical core we defer.

\subsection{Synthetic Validation}
\label{sec:synthetic}

Before any deep network, we gave the hypothesis a testbed where every
quantity has a known closed-form answer: streams of two-dimensional
two-Gaussian data against a frozen classifier whose ideal boundary is
available analytically. On this testbed the direction our analysis
predicts to be output-invisible matches the analytical one to numerical
precision (agreement better than $1 - 10^{-6}$ in absolute cosine), and
the two experiments of Fig.~\ref{fig:synthetic-demo} probe the two arrows
of Fig.~\ref{fig:geometry-toy} directly. A drift of four standard
deviations along the predicted invisible direction changes the model's
outputs by less than $10^{-10}$ per sample --- the invisibility is real,
not approximate --- while the calibrated monitor raises an alarm on all
fifteen post-onset batches. An equally large drift along the visible
direction leaves the monitor at its noise floor, exactly as intended:
that drift is the kind output-based monitors already catch, and a
specialist should not duplicate their alarms. Over two hundred clean
batches the stationary false-alarm rate stays within its calibrated
budget. The verification suite behind these claims --- 38 tests covering
the theory's predictions and the monitor's behavior --- is committed
alongside the code and re-runs in seconds on a laptop CPU, so every
number in this paragraph can be checked on demand.

\subsection{Research Questions}

Three questions structure the work:

\begin{enumerate}
\item{\textbf{RQ1 (characterization).} For standard classifier
  architectures, what exactly can and cannot be detected by any monitor
  computed from the model's outputs on the arriving data? Can the
  boundary be stated exactly rather than empirically?}
\item{\textbf{RQ2 (mechanism).} Can the Fisher geometry of the deployed
  model be turned into a streaming monitor that is label-free, calibrated,
  and cheap --- requirements (1), (3), and (4) --- while remaining
  harm-oriented, requirement (2)?}
\item{\textbf{RQ3 (evidence).} On benchmarks where harmfulness is measured
  rather than assumed, does the geometric monitor track measured damage,
  and what does it add over both output-based baselines and simpler
  geometry-free ablations of itself?}
\end{enumerate}

RQ3's final clause reflects a discipline we want to hold ourselves to: a
new method must be compared not only against prior work but against the
simplest degradation of itself, so that the contribution of each
ingredient is isolated rather than asserted.

\subsection{Objectives and Expected Novelty}

Concretely, the project will deliver: a formal answer to RQ1 for softmax
classifiers; a monitor implementing RQ2, with an offline fitting phase on
the held-out set and an online phase whose per-batch cost is a small
linear-algebra operation; a calibration procedure making the false-alarm
budget an explicit operator-chosen parameter; and the RQ3 evaluation. The
expected novelty is the connection itself: prior work has the
harmful-shift problem without the geometry \cite{amoukou2024sequential} or
geometric machinery without the harmful/benign question
\cite{zhang2023score, pandey2025zdp}; FEATHER is built in the
intersection.

\section{Planned Evaluation and Feasibility}
\label{sec:plan}

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\runinhead{Datasets and Streams.} Rotated MNIST (the test set rotated in
fixed angular steps, giving a controllable incremental-drift dial) and
CIFAR-10-C \cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking} (19 corruption types $\times$
5 severities plus the clean set), each streamed in batches against a model
trained on the clean distribution. Multiple training seeds throughout, so
that every reported number carries a spread rather than a point estimate.
As a stretch goal, schedule permitting, we will add one naturally
occurring shift task from WILDS \cite{koh2021wilds} (Camelyon17 is the
likely pick: modest size, and a domain shift whose harm is genuinely
graded rather than synthetically induced), so that the harm-measurement
machinery is exercised at least once outside the corruption setting.

\runinhead{Harm Labeling.} As committed in Sect.~\ref{sec:problem},
episodes are labeled harmful or benign by their measured accuracy drop
against the clean episode, with an explicit gray zone between the two
thresholds that is excluded from detection scoring rather than silently
assigned to either side.

\runinhead{Baselines.} Representatives of each family in
Table~\ref{tab:families}: classical stream detectors via River
\cite{montiel2021river} where labels permit; confidence- and entropy-based
output statistics \cite{hendrycks2017baseline, garg2022atc} as the
label-free comparison; an error-proxy sequential detector in the spirit of
Amoukou et al.\ \cite{amoukou2024sequential} --- the closest prior framing
--- whose proxy can be fit on the same held-out set our monitor uses; and,
as the primary comparison, a variance-weighted feature-space monitor:
activation covariance in place of Fisher information in an otherwise
identical pipeline at identical calibration. This last baseline is the
discipline check of RQ3 --- it isolates whether task-aware weighting earns
its keep over the Mahalanobis-style density geometry of prior
feature-space methods \cite{lee2018mahalanobis}, which is the comparison
the proposal lives or dies on.

\runinhead{Metrics.} Synthetic corruptions tend to be visible to every
detector family at once, so threshold-free separation of harmful from
benign episodes (AUROC) may saturate and rank nothing; we report it for
completeness but treat \emph{false-alarm behavior on measured-benign
episodes at matched calibration} --- which the literature underweights
relative to detection power, and which does discriminate between monitors
even when detection saturates --- as the primary metric on these
benchmarks. Alongside both: wall-clock cost of the offline phase and the
per-batch online step, reported relative to the cost of inference
itself.

\subsection{Tools and Technology}

PyTorch and torchvision for models and data; NumPy for the monitor's
linear algebra (deliberately: the online step should not need a GPU);
River \cite{montiel2021river} for classical baselines; matplotlib for
figures; pytest for the verification suite. Everything runs on one
workstation with a single 24 GB GPU; nothing requires cluster-scale
compute. The repository is version-controlled together with the training
logs, and every figure and table is regenerated by committed scripts.

\subsection{Feasibility and Constraints}

The project is deliberately scoped to be achievable within one semester by
a two-person team. On \emph{data}: all benchmarks are public and
downloadable; no collection or annotation is needed. On \emph{compute}:
the benchmark models train in minutes on the available GPU, and the
monitoring computations are orders of magnitude cheaper than the
inference they observe, so the experimental budget is hours, not weeks.
On \emph{skills}: the theory sits in undergraduate linear algebra and
probability; the engineering is standard PyTorch. On \emph{schedule}:
the theory and the synthetic verification are already complete
(Sect.~\ref{sec:synthetic}), the training and monitoring infrastructure
is largely built, and the remaining critical path is benchmark execution
and writing, both parallelizable between the two team members. Table~\ref{tab:risks} lists the main risks
and the mitigations we planned for each.

\section{Expected Outcomes}
\label{sec:outcomes}

The deliverables are the formal characterization (RQ1); a tested,
documented monitoring library usable against any PyTorch classifier that
exposes its penultimate features (RQ2); the benchmark study, with every
figure and table regenerable from committed scripts and logs (RQ3); and
the full manuscript. One softer outcome matters to us as much: honesty
about negative space. Wherever the benchmarks fail to discriminate
between methods we will say so and explain why, and we hope two
evaluation habits --- measure harm instead of assuming it, and grade
false-alarm discipline alongside detection power --- outlive the specific
method.

\section{Future Directions}
\label{sec:future}

Beyond the semester, two directions look promising. First, benchmarks:
corruption suites mostly produce shifts every monitor family can see;
building benchmarks for the \emph{silent} regime --- realistic drift that
leaves model outputs untouched, in the spirit of the naturally occurring
shifts cataloged by WILDS \cite{koh2021wilds} --- would give the
community a sharper instrument. Second, scope: the geometric viewpoint
should extend beyond the classifier head toward deeper layers, where
Kronecker-factored approximations \cite{martens2015kfac} make the
relevant objects tractable, and toward the representational-drift
questions now being asked of large language models \cite{pandey2025zdp}.
Both are outside the current project; we flag them as its natural
continuations.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Monitoring a deployed model without labels currently means choosing
between alarms about everything and trust in a witness with documented
reliability problems. We have reviewed the landscape, argued that the
missing ingredient is the geometry of the deployed model itself, and
introduced FEATHER: a project that turns this observation into a formal
account of what output-based monitoring can never see, a practical
streaming monitor built on the model's Fisher information, and an
evaluation that takes harmful-versus-benign --- and the discipline of
false alarms --- seriously. The construction, its guarantees, and the
measured results belong to the full manuscript; the problem, the gap, the
plan, and the feasibility case are, we hope, made here.
