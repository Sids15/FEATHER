\abstract*{Machine learning models lose accuracy after deployment because
the data they see keeps changing, and the labels that would reveal the
damage arrive late or never. The label-free monitors available today sit
at two extremes. Some alarm on every change in the input distribution,
whether it hurts the model or not. Others judge the model's health from
its own outputs (confidence, entropy, uncertainty), even though these
signals become unreliable under the shifts they are supposed to
catch. This paper introduces FEATHER (Fisher-Eigenvalue Adaptive
Thresholding for Error-Robust Drift Detection), an ongoing project that
starts instead from the geometry of the deployed model. Rather than
asking whether the data changed, it asks whether the change happened
somewhere the model can respond to at all. We review the concept-drift
problem, survey the main families of monitoring methods and the gap
between them, formulate the harmful-drift detection problem we target,
and present our research questions, evaluation plan, feasibility
analysis, and preliminary results on a synthetic testbed where every
prediction of the hypothesis can be checked against exact answers.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A classifier that was accurate the day it shipped does not stay accurate
for free. Production data drifts. User behavior changes, sensors get
replaced, lighting varies, vocabulary moves on, fraud adapts. Some of
this the model shrugs off, and some of it quietly breaks it.
Practitioners have known this for a long time. Sculley et al.\
\cite{sculley2015debt} listed changing input distributions among the
main sources of technical debt in production machine learning systems
back in 2015. The problem now has its own literature under the name
\emph{concept drift} \cite{gama2014survey, lu2018learning}.

How much this matters depends on the application. A recommendation model
that decays costs engagement. A credit-risk model costs money, and
possibly a regulator's attention. A medical triage or driver-assistance
model can cost far more. What makes these failures dangerous is their
silence more than their size. A deployed model does not crash when its
assumptions expire. It keeps producing predictions that look like
correct ones, and until someone notices, it is confidently wrong at
scale.

Detecting change as such is not the hard part. Sequential change
detectors go back to Page's CUSUM chart from 1954 \cite{page1954cusum},
and the stream-mining community has been refining them for learning
systems for two decades \cite{gama2004learning, baena2006eddm,
bifet2007adwin, raab2020kswin}. The hard part is that they answer the
wrong question. Nobody operating a model really wants to know whether
the data changed; in a real stream it changes constantly. What the
operator wants to know is whether the model still works, and the labels
needed to answer that directly arrive late, sparsely, or never. A loan
default takes months to observe. A misrouted support ticket gets fixed
downstream by a person who has no way to tell the model. So every
practical monitor is a proxy for accuracy. We think the choice of proxy
is where current practice has an unexamined weakness.

This paper introduces an ongoing research project called FEATHER
(Fisher-Eigenvalue Adaptive Thresholding for Error-Robust drift
detection). Its scope is deliberately limited: the method's
construction, its theory, and the deep-network results belong to the
full manuscript we are preparing, not here. What this paper does is set
up the ground the project stands on. Section~\ref{sec:background}
explains what concept drift is and why the harmful/benign distinction is
the one that matters in operation. Section~\ref{sec:survey} reviews the
existing families of monitors and the gap between them.
Section~\ref{sec:problem} states the problem and the requirements a good
solution has to meet. Section~\ref{sec:project} presents the proposed
methodology, from guiding hypothesis to evaluation plan and feasibility.
Section~\ref{sec:synthetic} reports preliminary results on a synthetic
testbed, and Sect.~\ref{sec:outcomes} discusses expected outcomes and
what lies beyond the project.

\section{Background: What Concept Drift Is}
\label{sec:background}

Let $X$ denote the model's input and $Y$ the target. A supervised model
is trained on samples from a joint distribution $P(X, Y)$;
\emph{dataset shift} is any change in that distribution between training
and serving \cite{quinonero2009dataset}. The literature organizes it
into three types. Under \emph{covariate shift} the input marginal
$P(X)$ changes but the concept $P(Y \mid X)$ does not; think of a new
camera sensor or a shifting user base. Under \emph{label shift} the
class proportions $P(Y)$ change while each class looks the same. A
flu-season surge changes how many patients have the flu, not what the
flu looks like \cite{lipton2018label}. Under \emph{concept shift} the
mapping $P(Y \mid X)$ moves: the transaction pattern that was
legitimate last year is fraudulent this year. Real streams mix all
three. There is also the question of how the change unfolds in time
(suddenly, gradually, incrementally, or in recurring cycles
\cite{gama2014survey, lu2018learning}). A detector tuned for abrupt
change points can miss a slow slide entirely.

These taxonomies classify the data. An operator cares about something
else: did the shift degrade the model? The two questions do not line up
well. A large, obvious covariate shift may cost a robust model nothing,
while a statistically subtle shift aimed at a sensitive part of the
decision boundary can be devastating. Following recent usage
\cite{amoukou2024sequential}, we call a shift \emph{harmful} when it
materially degrades the deployed model's accuracy and \emph{benign} when
it does not. We build the project around this distinction rather
than around the covariate/label/concept taxonomy. There is empirical
support for doing so: Rabanser et al.\ \cite{rabanser2019failing} showed
that how strongly a change is detected correlates poorly with how much
damage it causes. A monitor that says ``change detected'' and nothing
more leaves the hard part to whoever reads the alert.

Retraining does not remove the need for monitoring either. It needs the
labels the stream lacks, it tells nobody that an incident happened, and
it does nothing for the predictions served before the retrain.
Monitoring and adaptation are complements, not substitutes
\cite{lu2018learning}.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:survey}

Existing approaches split into three families according to the signal
they watch: the labels (when available), the inputs, or the model's
outputs. Table~\ref{tab:families} summarizes the comparison.

\subsection{Error-Rate Detectors: The Right Question, the Wrong Input}

The classical stream-mining detectors watch the model's error rate
directly, borrowing from statistical process control
\cite{page1954cusum}. DDM raises an alarm when the online error rate
climbs a significance margin above its running minimum
\cite{gama2004learning}. EDDM adapts the idea to gradual drift
\cite{baena2006eddm}. ADWIN keeps an adaptive window and cuts it when
two sub-windows disagree, with formal false-positive guarantees
\cite{bifet2007adwin}. These methods ask the right question (is the
model failing?) but consume the one resource deployment withholds, a
label for every prediction. With delayed labels they turn silent failure
into slow-motion failure, which is not the same thing as timely
detection. Mature implementations ship in the River library
\cite{montiel2021river}, which also makes them convenient baselines.

\subsection{Input-Distribution Tests: Sensitive but Indiscriminate}

A second family drops the label requirement and instead tests whether
the arriving inputs still look like the reference distribution. The
tools are two-sample tests: kernel statistics such as Maximum Mean
Discrepancy \cite{gretton2012kernel}, windowed Kolmogorov--Smirnov tests
as in KSWIN \cite{raab2020kswin}, and reduced-dimension variants that
test the model's representations instead of raw pixels
\cite{rabanser2019failing}. For structured cases such as label shift
there are dedicated estimators \cite{lipton2018label}.

Sensitivity is this family's strength, but it is also the source of its
weakness. The statistic measures distance between distributions,
not consequences for the model. Rabanser et al.\
\cite{rabanser2019failing} found that such detectors fire reliably on
synthetic corruptions, including the harmless ones. An operator who is
paged for every seasonal wobble stops reading the pages, and unread
alarms protect nothing. The same study names the characterization of
harmful shift as an open problem. That opening is the one this project
aims at.

\subsection{Output-Based Estimators: The Right Question, a Compromised Witness}

The newest family estimates the model's health from its own outputs.
The maximum-softmax-probability baseline showed that a classifier's
confidence carries usable signal about its correctness
\cite{hendrycks2017baseline}. Later work sharpened this into accuracy
estimates, using confidence differences \cite{guillory2021doc}, a
learned confidence threshold whose exceedance rate tracks accuracy
\cite{garg2022atc}, or a prediction-uncertainty index that reacts
earlier than error-rate methods \cite{pudd2025}. Closest to our framing,
Amoukou et al.\ \cite{amoukou2024sequential} formalize sequential
harmful-shift detection without labels: an error-proxy model scores the
stream and a sequential test controls the false-alarm rate. The
statistics community has meanwhile brought process-control charts to
model monitoring through score vectors \cite{zhang2023score}.

This family finally asks the operator's question, and often answers it
well. The trouble is the witness. Deep networks are systematically
miscalibrated even in distribution \cite{guo2017calibration}, and
calibration gets worse under the distribution shift a monitor is
meant to catch \cite{ovadia2019trust}. A sensor that is least reliable
when it is most needed is an uncomfortable thing to build on.

There is a further concern, separate from calibration, that we think has
not been examined carefully. Any statistic computed from a model's
outputs on the arriving data is, by construction, unable to notice what
the outputs do not express. Defining harm through accuracy does not
resolve this, because accuracy depends on the predictions and on the
ground-truth labels. A stream can move in a way that freezes every
prediction while the correct answers underneath keep changing, and a
monitor blind to that movement is blind where failure is silent.
Is the region of ``inexpressible'' change a negligible technicality, or
is it large and structured? That is a precise mathematical question
about the architecture. It is where our project starts, and we defer
everything further about it to the full manuscript.

\subsection{Feature-Space Methods and Benchmarks}

Out-of-distribution detection offers a related line that works in the
model's penultimate feature space. The Mahalanobis method scores samples
against class-conditional Gaussians fitted to features
\cite{lee2018mahalanobis}, and representation-level two-sample tests
were the strongest detectors in \cite{rabanser2019failing}. Feature
space is the right place to look, since it is where the model summarizes
what it believes about an input. But these methods weight directions by
variance or density rather than by relevance to the task, so they
inherit the performance-agnosticism of the input tests.

On evaluation, the field leans on corruption suites, above all
CIFAR-10-C with its 19 corruption types at 5 severities
\cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking}. Rotated-digit streams appear at
smaller scale, and WILDS covers naturally occurring shift
\cite{koh2021wilds}. One
limitation will matter later: corruption benchmarks grade detectability,
not consequence. The corruption name says nothing about whether a given
model actually loses accuracy. This is why we insist on measuring
harmfulness instead of assuming it.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}

We can now state the problem. A frozen classifier $f$ is deployed.
Unlabeled batches arrive as a stream. A labeled held-out set from the
training-time distribution is available once, offline, before
deployment. The monitor must decide, batch by batch, whether the stream
has drifted in a way that plausibly degrades $f$'s accuracy. It must
also stay quiet under drift that $f$ tolerates.

One commitment up front about ground truth. In our experiments the
harmfulness of an episode is \emph{measured} by scoring the deployed
model on held-out labels for that episode. It is never assumed from the
name or severity of the perturbation. A shift that looks dramatic but
costs no accuracy counts as benign. A subtle one that costs a lot counts
as harmful. This protects the evaluation from the benchmark limitation
noted in Sect.~\ref{sec:survey}.

From this review we draw four requirements for a satisfying monitor:

\begin{enumerate}
\item{\textbf{Label-free online.} After the one-time offline phase the
  monitor sees only unlabeled batches.}
\item{\textbf{Harm-oriented.} Alarms must be tied to the model's
  expected performance, not to distribution distance for its own sake.}
\item{\textbf{Calibrated.} The operator should choose the stationary
  false-alarm rate in advance; it should not fall out of hand-tuned
  constants. A monitor that cries wolf gets muted.}
\item{\textbf{Cheap.} Monitoring has to cost a small fraction of the
  inference it watches, or production teams will switch it off under
  load.}
\end{enumerate}

No family in Table~\ref{tab:families} meets all four. Error-rate charts
fail (1). Input tests fail (2). Output-based estimators meet (1) and (2)
but inherit the reliability problems documented in
\cite{guo2017calibration, ovadia2019trust}, along with the structural
concern raised above.

\section{Proposed Methodology}
\label{sec:project}

\subsection{Guiding Hypothesis}

FEATHER is our attempt to build a monitor around the deployed model's
own geometry rather than around its raw data or its final outputs. The
intuition is simple. A trained classifier does not use its internal
representation uniformly: some directions in feature space carry the
information its decisions depend on, and others provably do not. Suppose
that division can be pinned down for the specific deployed
model at hand, rather than statistically for models in general. Then
drift can be classified by \emph{where it lands} relative to what the
model is sensitive to. That is the harm-oriented criterion of
requirement (2), the one neither input tests nor output statistics
provide.

The tool we use is the Fisher information of the model, a classical
object from information geometry \cite{amari1998natural}. We compute it
in the empirical, label-conditioned form that the single offline
reference set makes available. The distinction from the true Fisher is
well documented \cite{kunstner2019empirical} and does not affect the
structural property we rely on. Fisher information already
plays a central role in optimization \cite{martens2015kfac} and in the
analysis of deep networks, whose Fisher spectra concentrate in a few
directions \cite{karakida2019universal}. It also matters in continual
learning, where it decides which parameters are too important to move
\cite{kirkpatrick2017overcoming}. Its appeal for monitoring is the same
as in those fields: it measures how sensitive the model's predictions
are to change, and that is what the harmful/benign distinction is about.
Geometric ideas have started to appear in the monitoring literature,
from score-vector control charts \cite{zhang2023score} to null-direction
analyses of language-model representations \cite{pandey2025zdp}. The
direction is timely. Label-free detectors that read network activations
directly, without any task supervision, exist as well
\cite{komorniczak2024parallel}. As far as we know, though, no existing
method connects this geometry to the harmful-versus-benign distinction
operators actually need, with the calibration and cost of requirements
(3) and~(4).

We keep the construction at this level of description on purpose. The
precise formulation, the guarantees we can prove, and our experimental
findings belong to the full manuscript in preparation. This paper's job
is to establish the problem, the gap, and the plan.

\subsection{A First Look, in Pictures}
\label{sec:glimpse}

Figure~\ref{fig:geometry-toy} shows the smallest example that carries
the idea. A frozen two-class classifier lives in a two-dimensional
feature space, and the dashed line is its decision boundary. Now let the
data drift. Along the red arrow, the classes get pushed across the
boundary: predictions, confidence, and entropy all react, and every
output-based monitor of Sect.~\ref{sec:survey} has something to work
with. Along the violet arrow the data moves just as far, but parallel to
the boundary. Not one prediction changes. Confidence and entropy read
as before. To every output statistic the world looks healthy,
however far the data travels.

The two arrows are not drawn by hand. They are computed from the frozen
model by the Fisher-information analysis at the heart of the project. In
two dimensions the split looks like a curiosity. Whether it matters
in practice comes down to two questions: how large the invisible region
is for real architectures, and whether damaging drift actually lands in
it. The first has an exact and, we think, surprising answer, which RQ1
formalizes and the full manuscript proves. The second is what the
benchmark evaluation measures. Both answers stay out of this paper on
purpose. The figure shows \emph{what} we exploit, not \emph{how} we
compute it for a real network, which is the technical core we defer.

\subsection{Research Questions}

Three questions structure the work:

\begin{enumerate}
\item{\textbf{RQ1 (characterization).} For standard classifier
  architectures, what can and cannot be detected by any monitor
  computed from the model's outputs on the arriving data? Can the
  boundary be stated exactly rather than empirically?}
\item{\textbf{RQ2 (mechanism).} Can the Fisher geometry of the deployed
  model be turned into a streaming monitor that is label-free,
  calibrated, and cheap (requirements 1, 3, and 4) while remaining
  harm-oriented (requirement 2)?}
\item{\textbf{RQ3 (evidence).} On benchmarks where harmfulness is
  measured rather than assumed, does the geometric monitor track
  measured damage, and what does it add over output-based baselines and
  over simpler geometry-free ablations of itself?}
\end{enumerate}

Concretely, the project will deliver a formal answer to RQ1 for softmax
classifiers; a monitor implementing RQ2 whose per-batch cost is a small
linear-algebra operation; a calibration procedure that makes the
false-alarm budget an operator-chosen parameter; and the RQ3 evaluation.
The expected novelty is the connection. Prior work has the
harmful-shift problem without the geometry \cite{amoukou2024sequential},
or the geometric machinery without the harmful/benign question
\cite{zhang2023score, pandey2025zdp}. FEATHER is built where the two
meet.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\runinhead{Datasets and Streams.} Rotated MNIST (the test set rotated in
fixed angular steps, which gives a controllable incremental-drift dial)
and CIFAR-10-C \cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking} (19 corruption types
$\times$ 5 severities plus the clean set), each streamed in batches
against a model trained on the clean distribution. Multiple training
seeds throughout, so every reported number carries a spread rather than
a point estimate. As a stretch goal, schedule permitting, we will add
one naturally occurring shift task from WILDS \cite{koh2021wilds}.
Camelyon17 is the likely pick: it is modest in size, and its harm is
graded rather than synthetically induced.

\runinhead{Harm Labeling.} As committed in Sect.~\ref{sec:problem},
episodes are labeled harmful or benign by their measured accuracy drop
against the clean episode. Episodes in the gray zone between the two
thresholds are excluded from detection scoring rather than silently
assigned to either side.

\runinhead{Baselines.} We compare against a representative of each
family in Table~\ref{tab:families}. Classical stream detectors, via
River \cite{montiel2021river}, are used where labels permit.
Confidence- and entropy-based output statistics \cite{hendrycks2017baseline,
garg2022atc} serve as the label-free comparison. An error-proxy
sequential detector, in the spirit of Amoukou et al.\
\cite{amoukou2024sequential} and the closest prior framing, has its
proxy fit on the same labeled calibration data our monitor uses. The
primary comparison is a variance-weighted feature-space monitor that
swaps activation covariance in for Fisher information, in an otherwise
identical pipeline at identical calibration. This baseline asks one
question: does task-aware weighting beat the plain density geometry of
earlier methods \cite{lee2018mahalanobis}? That is the comparison we
care about most. Calibration follows a held-out protocol throughout.
The reference data is split deterministically: one half fits each
monitor's geometry, and the other, disjoint half supplies its
thresholds. So no threshold is calibrated on the data that shaped the
statistic it gates.

\runinhead{Metrics.} Synthetic corruptions tend to be visible to every
detector family at once, so threshold-free separation of harmful from
benign episodes (AUROC) may saturate and rank nothing. We report it for
completeness. But the primary metric on these benchmarks is
\emph{false-alarm behavior on measured-benign episodes at matched
calibration}. The literature underweights this metric, and it keeps
discriminating when detection saturates. Alongside both: wall-clock cost
of the offline fit and the per-batch online step, relative to the cost
of inference.

\subsection{Tools and Technology}

PyTorch and torchvision for models and data. NumPy for the monitor's
linear algebra, deliberately, so the online step never needs a GPU.
River \cite{montiel2021river} for the classical baselines, matplotlib
for figures, pytest for the verification suite. Everything runs on one
workstation with a single 24\,GB GPU; nothing requires cluster-scale
compute. The repository is version-controlled together with the training
logs, and every figure and table is regenerated by committed scripts.

In line with the conference guidelines on research tools, we disclose
that an LLM-based coding assistant (Claude, Anthropic) was used to help
scaffold code and edit prose. The hypothesis, theory, experimental
design, and interpretation are the authors' own. Every number and figure
is produced by committed scripts the authors verified, and the suite of
Sect.~\ref{sec:synthetic} tests the implementation against analytic
ground truth, not against the assistant's output.

\subsection{Feasibility and Constraints}

The project is scoped to fit one semester and a two-person team. Data:
all benchmarks are public; nothing needs collecting or annotating.
Compute: the benchmark models train in minutes on the available GPU, and
monitoring is far cheaper than the inference it observes, so the
experimental budget is hours rather than weeks. Skills: the theory sits
in undergraduate linear algebra and probability, and the engineering is
standard PyTorch. Schedule: the theory and the synthetic verification
are complete (Sect.~\ref{sec:synthetic}). The training and monitoring
infrastructure is built, and the benchmark execution on both suites has
finished, including the output-baseline comparison (five seeds per
model, held-out calibration throughout). What remains is the WILDS
stretch goal and the writing, which can be split between the two team
members.

Four risks dominate, each with a planned mitigation. The theory might
fail to transfer from the synthetic setting to deep networks. The
synthetic gate of Sect.~\ref{sec:synthetic} catches that early, since
every prediction is verified analytically before deep models enter, and
we will report the transfer honestly either way. The benchmarks might
saturate, with every detector succeeding. The pre-declared primary
metric (false-alarm discipline at matched calibration) and the
covariance baseline keep discriminating in that case, and the WILDS
stretch task adds a non-synthetic setting. The completed runs bore this
out: threshold-free detection did saturate for every detector, while the
primary metric still separated FEATHER from its covariance ablation.
This held even when FEATHER's own reference was calibrated on the
deployment stream, symmetrically with the output baselines, so the margin
is not an artifact of a privileged reference (the measured numbers belong
to the full manuscript). Compute shortfalls are
contained by small models and checkpoint-resume everywhere, so an
interrupted run loses minutes, not days. Timeline pressure in the
writing stage is absorbed by script-generated figures and tables, which
let late results flow into the manuscript without manual rework.

\section{Preliminary Results: Synthetic Validation}
\label{sec:synthetic}

Before any deep network, we gave the hypothesis a testbed where every
quantity has a known closed-form answer: streams of two-dimensional
two-Gaussian data against a frozen classifier whose ideal boundary is
available analytically. On this testbed, the direction our analysis
predicts to be output-invisible matches the analytical one to numerical
precision, with agreement better than $1 - 10^{-6}$ in absolute cosine.
The two experiments of Fig.~\ref{fig:synthetic-demo} then probe the two
arrows of Fig.~\ref{fig:geometry-toy} directly. A drift of four standard
deviations along the predicted invisible direction changes the model's
outputs by less than $10^{-10}$ per sample, so the invisibility is real
rather than approximate. Yet the calibrated monitor raises an alarm on
all fifteen post-onset batches. An equally large drift along the visible
direction leaves the monitor at its noise floor, as intended,
since that drift is the kind output-based monitors already catch. Over
two hundred clean batches, the stationary false-alarm rate stays within
its calibrated budget. The verification suite behind these claims now
covers 69 tests: the theory's predictions, the monitor's behavior,
the held-out calibration protocol, and the output baselines. It is
committed alongside the code and re-runs in seconds on a laptop CPU, so
every number in this paragraph can be checked on demand.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:outcomes}

Beyond the concrete deliverables of Sect.~\ref{sec:project} (the
characterization, the monitoring library, the benchmark study, and the
full manuscript), one softer outcome matters to us as much: honesty
about negative space. Wherever the benchmarks fail to discriminate
between methods, we will say so and explain why. We also hope two
evaluation habits outlive the specific method: measure harm instead of
assuming it, and grade false-alarm discipline alongside detection power.

Looking past the semester, two directions seem promising. The first is
benchmarks. Corruption suites mostly produce shifts every monitor family
can see, so building benchmarks for the \emph{silent} regime (realistic
drift that leaves model outputs untouched, in the spirit of the
naturally occurring shifts cataloged by WILDS \cite{koh2021wilds}) would
give the community a sharper instrument. The second is scope. The
geometric viewpoint should extend beyond the classifier head toward
deeper layers, where Kronecker-factored approximations
\cite{martens2015kfac} make the relevant objects tractable. It should
also extend toward the representational-drift questions now being asked
of large language models \cite{pandey2025zdp}. Both sit outside the
current project; we
flag them as its natural continuations.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Label-free monitoring today means choosing between alarms about
everything and trust in a witness with documented reliability problems.
We have reviewed the landscape and argued that the missing ingredient is
the geometry of the deployed model. FEATHER is our attempt to
turn that observation into three things: a formal account of what
output-based monitoring can never see, a practical streaming monitor
built on the model's Fisher information, and an evaluation that takes
the harmful/benign distinction and false-alarm discipline seriously. The
construction, the guarantees, and the measured results belong to the
full manuscript. The problem, the gap, the plan, and the feasibility
case are, we hope, made here.
