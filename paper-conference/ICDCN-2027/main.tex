\begin{abstract}
A classifier's accuracy does not hold still once it is deployed. The input
data keeps moving, and the labels that would tell us the model has started
failing usually show up late, if they show up at all. Label-free monitors
try to fill that gap, but the ones we have tend to fall into one of two
camps. One camp raises an alarm whenever the input distribution moves,
harmful or not. The other reads the model's own outputs (confidence,
entropy, uncertainty) and infers health from them, which is awkward,
because those same outputs get unreliable under exactly the shifts a
monitor is there to catch. We take a different starting point. This paper
introduces FEATHER (Fisher-Eigenvalue Adaptive Thresholding for
Error-Robust Drift Detection), an ongoing project built around the
geometry of the deployed model rather than its data or its outputs. The
question it asks is not whether the data changed, but whether it changed
somewhere the model can actually react to. The paper reviews the
concept-drift problem, walks through the main families of monitoring
methods and where they fall short, states the harmful-drift problem we are
after, and lays out our research questions, evaluation plan, feasibility
case, and early results on a synthetic testbed where every claim can be
checked against an exact answer.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Accuracy on launch day is not accuracy forever. The data a deployed model
receives starts to change over time. Users may behave differently, a
sensor may be replaced, the lighting may change, the vocabulary that is
used may change, and fraud patterns can adapt to the methods that are used
to detect them. Some of these changes don't affect the model much, while
some changes can reduce the performance of the model and still remain
unnoticed. This problem is not new. Back in 2015, Sculley et al.\
\cite{sculley2015debt} identified the changing of input distributions as
one of the major sources of technical debt in production machine learning
systems. Today, this problem is commonly studied and known as
\emph{concept drift} \cite{gama2014survey, lu2018learning}.

The effect of drift also depends on where the model is being used. For
example, an outdated recommender system may result in lower user
engagement, while errors in a credit-risk model can lead to financial loss
and may also create regulatory concerns. A triage model in a hospital, or
the perception stack in a car, can cost a great deal more than that. The
dangerous thing, though, is not really the size of the failure but how
quiet it is. A model whose assumptions have expired does not throw an
error. It keeps handing back predictions that look perfectly ordinary, and
it goes on being wrong, at scale, for as long as it takes somebody to
notice.

Spotting change, on its own, is not the difficult part. Sequential change
detectors have been around since Page's CUSUM chart in 1954
\cite{page1954cusum}, and for the last twenty years or so the
stream-mining community has been adapting them to learning systems
\cite{gama2004learning, baena2006eddm, bifet2007adwin, raab2020kswin}.
The difficulty is that ``did the data change?'' is the wrong question. In
any real stream the data is always changing. What the person running the
model actually wants to know is whether the model still works, and the
labels that would answer that show up late, or only in dribs and drabs, or
never. A loan takes months to default. A support ticket that got routed to
the wrong queue is quietly fixed by whoever it lands on, who has no channel
back to the model. Because those labels are missing, every monitor you can
actually deploy is standing in for accuracy rather than measuring it. Our
claim is that the field has not looked hard enough at which stand-in it
picks.

This paper is the introductory piece of an ongoing project we call
FEATHER (Fisher-Eigenvalue Adaptive Thresholding for Error-Robust drift
detection). We have kept its scope narrow on purpose. How the method is
actually built, the theory behind it, and the results on deep networks all
belong to the full manuscript we are still writing, not to this paper.
What we do here is lay the ground the project rests on.
Section~\ref{sec:background} says what concept drift is and why, in
operation, the split that matters is harmful versus benign rather than the
textbook taxonomy. Section~\ref{sec:survey} goes through the existing
monitor families and the gap they leave open. Section~\ref{sec:problem}
pins down the problem and what we would demand of any decent solution.
Section~\ref{sec:project} explains the methodology, including the guiding
hypothesis, evaluation plan, and feasibility of the project.
Section~\ref{sec:synthetic} presents the initial results from the
synthetic testbed, while Section~\ref{sec:outcomes} discusses the findings
and future directions.

\section{Background: What Concept Drift Is}
\label{sec:background}

Let the model input be represented by $X$ and the target be represented by
$Y$. A supervised model learns from samples that follow a joint
distribution $P(X, Y)$. Any change in this distribution between the
training and deployment stages is referred to as \emph{dataset shift}
\cite{quinonero2009dataset}. It usually gets split into three kinds. In
\emph{covariate shift} the input distribution $P(X)$ changes while
$P(Y \mid X)$ remains the same. For example, this can happen because of a
new camera sensor or a different group of users. In \emph{label shift} the
class distribution $P(Y)$ changes while the classes themselves remain
similar. For example, the number of flu patients increases during the flu
season, but the characteristics of flu do not change
\cite{lipton2018label}. In \emph{concept shift} $P(Y \mid X)$ itself
changes. For example, a transaction that was considered legitimate earlier
may now be considered as a fraud. In data streams of the real-world, these
different types of shifts can also happen together. Another important
factor that plays into effect is how the shift happens over time. It can
be abrupt, gradual, incremental, or recurring \cite{gama2014survey,
lu2018learning}. Because of this, a detector designed mainly for sudden
changes may fail to detect a slow drift. Figure~\ref{fig:drift-types}
shows these four profiles side by side.

These categories tell us what type of change has happened in the data, but
they do not tell us whether that change actually affects the model. A
large covariate shift may have almost no effect on a robust model. At the
same time, a much smaller shift near a sensitive part of the decision
boundary may cause a large drop in performance. Following the definition
used in recent work \cite{amoukou2024sequential}, we consider a shift
\emph{harmful} when it causes a measurable drop in the accuracy of the
deployed model, and \emph{benign} when it does not. Our project focuses
mainly on this harmful-versus-benign difference, rather than only
separating shifts into covariate, label, and concept shift. There is
evidence this is the right call: Rabanser et al.\ \cite{rabanser2019failing}
found that how loudly a shift is detected has little to do with how much
damage it does. A monitor that reports ``something changed'' and stops
there has handed the actual work to whoever is on call.

Retraining is not a way out of this, either. It wants the very labels the
stream is not giving you, it does not tell anyone that something went
wrong, and it does nothing about the predictions already served before the
new model went in. Monitoring and retraining sit alongside each other; one
does not replace the other \cite{lu2018learning}.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:survey}

The existing work sorts fairly cleanly into three families, by which
signal each one watches: the labels (when you have them), the inputs, or
the model's own outputs. Table~\ref{tab:families} lays the three side by
side.

\subsection{Error-Rate Detectors: The Right Question, the Wrong Input}

The classical stream-mining detectors go straight at the model's error
rate, an idea lifted from statistical process control \cite{page1954cusum}.
DDM fires once the online error rate climbs a set margin above its running
minimum \cite{gama2004learning}; EDDM reworks the same idea for drift that
comes on gradually \cite{baena2006eddm}; ADWIN holds an adaptive window and
chops it whenever two halves of that window start to disagree, and it comes
with real false-positive guarantees \cite{bifet2007adwin}. These are all
asking the right thing, is the model failing, but to do it they need the
one thing deployment refuses to hand over, which is a label for every
single prediction. When the labels are late, all this really buys you is
that silent failure turns into slow-motion failure, and slow-motion
failure is not the same as catching the problem in time. The River library
\cite{montiel2021river} ships solid implementations, which at least makes
them easy baselines to run against.

\subsection{Input-Distribution Tests: Sensitive but Indiscriminate}

The second family gives up on labels and asks instead whether the incoming
inputs still resemble the reference data. These methods usually use
two-sample testing to detect changes between distributions. This includes
kernel-based methods such as Maximum Mean Discrepancy
\cite{gretton2012kernel}, window-based Kolmogorov--Smirnov tests such as
KSWIN \cite{raab2020kswin}, and lower-dimensional methods that perform the
test on model representations instead of directly using raw pixels
\cite{rabanser2019failing}. For shifts with a specific structure, such as
label shift, separate estimators have also been developed
\cite{lipton2018label}.

The sensitivity of these methods is useful for detecting distribution
changes, but it also creates a limitation. The test measures how different
two distributions are, but it does not measure how much that difference
affects the model. Rabanser et al.\ \cite{rabanser2019failing} showed that
these detectors can reliably detect synthetic corruptions, including the
ones that do not actually harm the model. If a monitoring system gives
alarms for every small or harmless change, these alarms can eventually
become less useful in practice. The same work identifies the
characterization of harmful shifts as an open problem, and this is the
problem that we focus on in this project.

\subsection{Output-Based Estimators: The Right Question, a Compromised Witness}

The most recent group of methods uses the model's own outputs to monitor
its performance. One of the starting points was the
maximum-softmax-probability baseline, which showed that the confidence of
a classifier gives useful information about whether its prediction is
correct \cite{hendrycks2017baseline}. After this, different methods started
using confidence to estimate accuracy. Some use differences in confidence
\cite{guillory2021doc}, some use a learned confidence threshold where the
exceedance rate is used to track accuracy \cite{garg2022atc}, and others
use a prediction-uncertainty index that can change before the error rate
starts changing \cite{pudd2025}. The work closest to our problem is
Amoukou et al.\ \cite{amoukou2024sequential}, where harmful shifts are
detected sequentially without labels. They use an error proxy to score the
incoming stream and a sequential test to control the false-alarm rate.
Meanwhile the statistics side has carried process-control charts over to
model monitoring by way of score vectors \cite{zhang2023score}.

This is the first family that actually asks the operator's question, and a
lot of the time it answers it well. The problem is the witness it relies
on. Deep networks are miscalibrated in a fairly systematic way even on
in-distribution data \cite{guo2017calibration}, and the calibration only
gets worse under precisely the distribution shift the monitor exists to
catch \cite{ovadia2019trust}. Building on a sensor that is at its worst
exactly when you need it most is an uneasy place to stand.

There is a second problem, separate from calibration, and we do not think
it has been looked at closely. The main limitation is that a method using
only the model outputs cannot detect information that is not present in
those outputs. As accuracy depends on both the model predictions and the
true labels, defining harm using accuracy does not completely solve this
problem. Consider a stream where the predictions remain the same even
after a shift, but the actual correct labels start changing. A monitor
which only looks at the outputs will not detect this change, even though
the model may now be making errors. This raises an important question: is
this region of change that cannot be expressed through the outputs very
small, or does it have some larger structure? This is a question that can
be studied directly from the model architecture. It is also where our
project starts, while the complete analysis is kept for the full
manuscript.

\subsection{Feature-Space Methods and Benchmarks}

Another related approach comes from out-of-distribution detection, where
monitoring is done using the model's penultimate feature space. The
Mahalanobis method scores samples using class-conditional Gaussian
distributions fitted in this feature space \cite{lee2018mahalanobis}.
Representation-level two-sample tests were also found to be among the
strongest detectors in Rabanser et al.\ \cite{rabanser2019failing}. Looking
at the feature space makes sense because the model has already converted
the input into features that it considers useful. However, these methods
mainly consider directions based on variance or density instead of how
important those directions are for the actual task. Because of this, they
can still be performance-agnostic in a similar way to raw input tests.

For evaluation, most of the field leans on corruption suites, CIFAR-10-C
above all, with its 19 corruption types at 5 severities each
\cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking}. Rotated-digit streams show up at smaller
scale, and WILDS supplies shift that occurs naturally rather than by
design \cite{koh2021wilds}. One thing about these benchmarks matters later
on: they grade how detectable a corruption is, not how much it costs. The
name on the corruption tells you nothing about whether this particular
model actually loses accuracy to it. That gap is the reason we measure
harmfulness directly instead of taking it for granted.

\subsection{Monitoring in Production ML Systems}

There is a systems side to this problem too, separate from the detector
math. Sculley et al.\ \cite{sculley2015debt} made the point that a deployed
model is only a small piece of a much larger system, and that its data
dependencies are where a lot of the long-term cost hides. Drift is one of
those dependencies. In practice the monitor is just one more service in a
pipeline. It reads the same stream the model reads, it has a compute
budget, and it competes with the model for that budget. Streaming toolkits
such as River \cite{montiel2021river} exist because this online, one-pass
setting is its own engineering problem and not only a modeling one.
Benchmarks built from naturally occurring shift, such as WILDS
\cite{koh2021wilds}, matter here for the same reason. They test a monitor
against the kind of drift a real pipeline meets, rather than a corruption
picked for being easy to spot. We keep this systems view in mind when we
set the cost and calibration requirements in the next section.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Operating Setting}

It helps to picture where this monitor actually sits. A trained model is
deployed behind a serving endpoint. Requests arrive as a continuous
stream, the model answers each one, and those answers feed straight into
some downstream product. Nothing pauses to say whether the answers are
still any good. Labels, when they arrive at all, come from a separate and
much slower loop. Take a fraud model as an example. A charge is scored in
milliseconds, but whether it was really fraud may only be known weeks
later, once a chargeback comes back. The monitor has to run inside the
fast loop, on the unlabeled stream, and it has to be light enough that
nobody minds leaving it switched on. This is the setting the rest of the
paper assumes.

With that background, here is the problem we set ourselves. A classifier
$f$ is deployed and frozen. Batches of unlabeled data come in as a stream.
Once, offline, before any of this starts, we get a single labeled held-out
set drawn from the training-time distribution. From then on the monitor
has to make a call batch by batch: has the stream drifted in a way that
would plausibly cost $f$ some accuracy? And, just as importantly, it has
to keep quiet when the drift is something $f$ can handle.

One thing we want to be firm about from the start is how ground truth gets
decided. In every experiment, whether an episode is harmful is
\emph{measured}, by scoring the deployed model on held-out labels for that
episode. We never read it off the name of the perturbation or how severe
it is supposed to be. A shift that looks dramatic but costs no accuracy is
benign as far as we are concerned; a quiet one that costs a lot is harmful.
That is what keeps the evaluation clear of the benchmark problem we pointed
out in Section~\ref{sec:survey}.

\subsection{Requirements}

Out of all this, four requirements fall out for a monitor worth having:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Label-free online.} Once the one-off offline phase is done,
  the monitor only ever sees unlabeled batches.
\item \textbf{Harm-oriented.} An alarm has to track the model's expected
  performance, not distance between distributions for its own sake.
\item \textbf{Calibrated.} The operator picks the steady-state
  false-alarm rate ahead of time; it should not be an accident of some
  hand-tuned constant. Fire too often and the monitor gets switched off.
\item \textbf{Cheap.} Monitoring has to cost only a small slice of the
  inference it is watching, or the team running things will turn it off
  the first time load spikes.
\end{enumerate}

None of the methods in Table~\ref{tab:families} satisfy all four
requirements. Error-rate charts do not satisfy requirement~(1), while
input-based tests do not satisfy requirement~(2). Output-based estimators
are able to satisfy both~(1) and~(2), but they still have the reliability
issues reported in \cite{guo2017calibration, ovadia2019trust}. They also
have the structural limitation discussed above, where some changes cannot
be detected from the model outputs alone.

\section{Proposed Methodology}
\label{sec:project}

\subsection{Guiding Hypothesis}

FEATHER is our try at building the monitor around the deployed model's own
geometry instead of around its raw data or its final outputs. The
intuition behind it is not complicated. A trained classifier does not lean
on its internal representation evenly: some directions in feature space
carry the information its decisions actually turn on, and others provably
carry none. Now suppose you could nail down that split for the one specific
model in front of you, rather than on average across models. Then you could
sort drift by \emph{where it lands} relative to what the model is sensitive
to. That sorting is the harm-oriented test of requirement~(2), the one that
neither the input tests nor the output statistics can give you.

The object we lean on is the model's Fisher information, a classical piece
of information geometry \cite{amari1998natural}. We use it in the
empirical, label-conditioned form, which is what a single offline reference
set actually lets you compute. The gap between this empirical version and
the true Fisher is well understood \cite{kunstner2019empirical}, and it
does not touch the structural property we depend on. Fisher information is
hardly exotic here: it is central to optimization \cite{martens2015kfac},
it shows up in the analysis of deep networks, whose Fisher spectra pile up
in a handful of directions \cite{karakida2019universal}, and it drives
continual learning, where it decides which weights are too important to
disturb \cite{kirkpatrick2017overcoming}. The reason it fits monitoring is
the same reason it fits those settings: it measures how sensitive the
model's predictions are to a change, and sensitivity is the whole point of
the harmful-versus-benign split. Geometry has started showing up in
monitoring, too, from score-vector control charts \cite{zhang2023score} to
null-direction analyses of language-model representations
\cite{pandey2025zdp}, so the timing is good. There are also label-free
detectors that read network activations directly, with no task supervision
at all \cite{komorniczak2024parallel}. Still, as far as we can tell, nobody
has tied this geometry to the harmful-versus-benign call that operators
actually need, and done it inside the calibration and cost limits of
requirements~(3) and~(4).

We are keeping the method at this level of detail for now. The exact
formulation, the guarantees that we can prove, and the experimental results
are part of the full manuscript which is currently in preparation. In this
paper, we mainly focus on explaining the problem, the existing gap, and how
we plan to address it.

\subsection{A First Look, in Pictures}
\label{sec:glimpse}

Figure~\ref{fig:geometry-toy} shows the idea using a simple example. We
take a frozen two-class classifier in a two-dimensional feature space, with
the dashed line showing its decision boundary, and then let the data drift.
When we move the data along the red arrow, the classes move across the
decision boundary. The predictions start changing and we also see changes
in confidence and entropy. This means the output-based monitors discussed
in Section~\ref{sec:survey} have changes in the outputs that they can
detect. Now we move the data by the same distance along the violet arrow,
which is parallel to the decision boundary. In this case, the model outputs
do not change. None of the predictions change, and the confidence and
entropy also remain exactly the same as before. Even if the data continues
moving in this direction, the output statistics will still show no change.

Both of these directions are calculated from the frozen model itself and
are not manually selected. They come from the Fisher-information analysis
used in this project. In two dimensions, this can be shown easily, but the
important question is whether the same idea is useful for real models. This
mainly depends on two things: how large this invisible region is for real
architectures, and whether harmful drift actually occurs inside this
region. The first question has an exact answer, which is covered by RQ1 and
proved in the full manuscript. The second question is tested through the
benchmark study. We intentionally do not include the answers to these two
questions in this paper.

\subsection{Research Questions}

Three questions run through the whole project:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{RQ1 (characterization).} For a standard classifier
  architecture, what can and cannot be picked up by any monitor built
  from the model's outputs on the incoming data? And can that boundary
  be written down exactly, not just estimated empirically?
\item \textbf{RQ2 (mechanism).} Can the Fisher geometry of the deployed
  model be turned into a streaming monitor that is label-free,
  calibrated, and cheap (requirements 1, 3, and 4) without giving up on
  being harm-oriented (requirement 2)?
\item \textbf{RQ3 (evidence).} On benchmarks where harmfulness is
  measured rather than assumed, does the geometric monitor actually
  track the measured damage, and what does it buy over output-based
  baselines and over stripped-down, geometry-free versions of itself?
\end{enumerate}

In concrete terms, the project will hand back four things: a formal answer
to RQ1 for softmax classifiers; a monitor for RQ2 whose per-batch cost is
one small linear-algebra step; a calibration routine that turns the
false-alarm budget into a knob the operator sets; and the RQ3 evaluation
itself. What we expect to be new is the join. Earlier work has the
harmful-shift problem but not the geometry \cite{amoukou2024sequential}, or
the geometric machinery but not the harmful-versus-benign question
\cite{zhang2023score, pandey2025zdp}. FEATHER sits at the point where the
two meet.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\paragraph{Datasets and Streams.} We start the evaluation with two
benchmarks. The first one is Rotated MNIST, where we rotate the test images
by fixed angles to create the drift step by step. The second one is
CIFAR-10-C \cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking}, which contains 19 different
corruption types with 5 severity levels, along with the clean data. For
both benchmarks, the data is given to a model in batches, while the model
itself is trained only on clean data. We also train the models using
different seeds instead of depending on one training run. This allows us to
report the variation between runs along with the final results. If we have
enough time, we also plan to test one natural distribution shift from WILDS
\cite{koh2021wilds}. Camelyon17 is currently the main option since the
dataset is manageable in size and the shift comes from real data rather
than an artificially created corruption.

\paragraph{Harm Labeling.} As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:problem}, each
episode is tagged harmful or benign by the accuracy it actually loses
relative to the clean episode. Episodes that fall in the gray band between
the two thresholds are dropped from detection scoring rather than quietly
shoved onto one side or the other.

\paragraph{Baselines.} We line up one representative from each family in
Table~\ref{tab:families}. The classical stream detectors, run through
River \cite{montiel2021river}, go in wherever labels are available.
Confidence- and entropy-based output statistics \cite{hendrycks2017baseline,
garg2022atc} are the label-free comparison. An error-proxy sequential
detector, built in the spirit of Amoukou et al.\
\cite{amoukou2024sequential} as the nearest prior framing, gets its proxy
fit on the same labeled calibration data our own monitor uses. The
comparison we care about most, though, is against a variance-weighted
feature-space monitor that keeps our whole pipeline intact but swaps
activation covariance in where we use Fisher information, at identical
calibration. That one baseline asks a single clean question: does weighting
directions by the task beat weighting them by plain density, the way
earlier methods did \cite{lee2018mahalanobis}? Calibration is held-out
throughout. We split the reference data deterministically, one half to fix
each monitor's geometry and the other, disjoint, half to set its
thresholds, so no threshold is ever calibrated on the same data that shaped
the statistic it is gating.

\paragraph{Metrics.} Synthetic corruptions have a way of being obvious to
every family of detector at the same time, so a threshold-free split of
harmful from benign episodes (AUROC) can saturate and end up ranking
nothing. We report it anyway, for completeness. The metric we actually lead
with on these benchmarks is \emph{how the monitor behaves on
measured-benign episodes at matched calibration}, its false-alarm
discipline, in other words. The literature tends to underweight it, and it
keeps telling methods apart well after the detection numbers have
saturated. On top of both, we time things: the wall-clock cost of the
offline fit and of the per-batch online step, set against the cost of
inference itself.

\subsection{Tools and Technology}

We are using PyTorch and torchvision for the models and data. NumPy is used
for the linear algebra required by the monitor. We chose NumPy here because
the online step can then run without using the GPU. River
\cite{montiel2021river} is used to run the classical baselines. We use
matplotlib for the figures and pytest for checking the implementation. The
experiments are running on one workstation with a 24\,GB GPU. This is
enough for the complete setup and we do not need a compute cluster. We also
keep the code and training logs under version control. Scripts for
generating the figures and tables are included in the repository as well.

We used Claude (Anthropic) as a coding assistant during the project and
also for some editing of the paper. The main idea and hypothesis were
decided by us. The theory, how the experiments should be performed, and the
final interpretation of the results were also done by us. We checked the
scripts used for generating all the numbers and figures ourselves. For
verification, the tests in Section~\ref{sec:synthetic} check our
implementation using the analytic ground truth. They do not use the results
generated by the coding assistant as the ground truth.

\subsection{Feasibility and Constraints}

The project can be completed in one semester with a team of two. All the
data we are using is publicly available, so there is no need for any new
data collection or annotation. The models used for the benchmarks also take
only a few minutes to train on our GPU. The monitor itself does not require
much computation compared to the model inference, so running all the
experiments should take a few hours and not weeks. The theory mainly
requires basic linear algebra and probability, and PyTorch is used for the
implementation. A good part of the work is already completed. The theory
and synthetic verification are done (Section~\ref{sec:synthetic}), and we
have also built the training and monitoring setup. The benchmark runs for
both suites are completed as well, including the output-baseline
comparison. We used five seeds for each model and kept the calibration data
separate during the experiments. The work that is mainly left now is the
WILDS stretch goal and writing the paper, which can be divided between the
two team members.

Four risks stand out, and each has a plan behind it. First, the theory
might not carry over from the synthetic setting to real deep networks. The
synthetic gate in Section~\ref{sec:synthetic} is there to catch that early,
since every prediction is checked analytically before a single deep model
is trained, and whichever way the transfer goes, we will report it
straight. Second, the benchmarks might saturate, with every detector doing
well. If that happens, the primary metric we committed to in advance
(false-alarm discipline at matched calibration) and the covariance baseline
both keep drawing distinctions, and the WILDS stretch task brings in a
setting that is not synthetic at all. The finished runs actually bore this
out: threshold-free detection did saturate across the board, and the
primary metric still pulled FEATHER apart from its covariance ablation, and
it did so even when FEATHER's reference was calibrated on the deployment
stream, the same way the output baselines are, so the gap is not just an
artifact of handing FEATHER a privileged reference (the measured numbers
are in the full manuscript). Third, we might run short on compute; small
models and checkpoint-resume everywhere mean an interrupted run costs us
minutes rather than days. Fourth, the writing could get squeezed at the
end, but since the figures and tables are all script-generated, late
results drop into the manuscript without anyone redoing them by hand.

\section{Preliminary Results: Synthetic Validation}
\label{sec:synthetic}

Before touching a deep network, we put the hypothesis on a testbed where
every quantity has a closed-form answer to check against: streams of
two-dimensional, two-Gaussian data run at a frozen classifier whose ideal
boundary we can write down analytically. On that testbed, the direction our
analysis says should be output-invisible lines up with the analytic one to
numerical precision, better than $1 - 10^{-6}$ in absolute cosine. The two
experiments in Figure~\ref{fig:synthetic-demo} test both the cases shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:geometry-toy}. For the first case, we apply a drift of four
standard deviations in the predicted invisible direction. Even after this,
the change in model outputs is less than $10^{-10}$ per sample. So
basically the outputs do not change, but our calibrated monitor is still
able to detect all fifteen batches after the drift starts. For the visible
direction, we apply the same amount of drift, but here the monitor stays
close to its normal noise level. This is expected since this type of drift
can already be detected using output-based methods. We also tested two
hundred clean batches to check the false alarms, and the false-alarm rate
stays within the calibrated budget. Currently, there are 69 tests in the
entire suite. These tests cover the theory predictions, monitor behavior,
held-out calibration, and the output baselines. The tests are also included
with the code and take only a few seconds to run on a normal laptop CPU, so
the reported results can be checked again easily.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:outcomes}

Apart from the main deliverables in Section~\ref{sec:project}, that include
the characterization, monitoring library, benchmark study, and the full
manuscript, another outcome we care about is being clear about the
limitations. Wherever the benchmarks are not able to differentiate between
methods, we intend to clearly mention it and explain why. We also hope that
two practices remain useful beyond this particular method: measuring the
actual harm instead of assuming it, and evaluating false alarms along with
the raw detection performance.

Looking beyond this semester, two directions seem worth exploring. The
first is the benchmarks. Corruption suites mostly provide shifts that
almost every type of monitor can detect, so a benchmark focused on the
\emph{silent} regime, where realistic drift occurs without changing the
model's outputs, similar to the natural shifts collected in WILDS
\cite{koh2021wilds}, could provide a better way to evaluate these methods.
The second is the scope. The geometric approach could be extended beyond
the classifier head to deeper layers, where Kronecker-factored
approximations \cite{martens2015kfac} can keep the computations manageable.
It could also be extended to study representational drift in large language
models \cite{pandey2025zdp}. Neither fits inside the current project; we
mark them as the natural next steps.

\subsection{Limitations and Threats to Validity}

A few limits are worth stating plainly. The strongest evidence in this
paper is synthetic, and a closed-form testbed is a friendly place for any
method. The deep-network results that would really test the idea sit in
the full manuscript, not here. The characterization we lean on is exact
for the softmax head we study, and we do not yet claim it carries over
unchanged to every architecture. The Fisher information we use is the
empirical, label-conditioned kind, which is what a single offline
reference set allows, and it is not the true Fisher
\cite{kunstner2019empirical}. We argue the gap does not touch the property
we depend on, but that argument belongs to the full manuscript as well.
Finally, all of our runs sit on a single workstation, so we report
variation across seeds rather than across large-scale deployments. None of
these sink the project, but a reader deserves to know where the evidence
is still thin.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

As things stand, label-free monitoring makes you pick between alarming at
everything and trusting a witness with a documented history of
unreliability. We have gone through the landscape and made the case that
what is missing from it is the geometry of the deployed model. FEATHER is
our attempt to turn that one observation into three concrete things: a
formal account of what output-based monitoring simply cannot see, a
streaming monitor light enough to run in practice and built on the model's
Fisher information, and an evaluation that takes both the
harmful-versus-benign split and false-alarm discipline seriously. The
construction, the guarantees, and the measured results are for the full
manuscript. The problem, the gap, the plan, and the case that all of it is
feasible are what we have tried to set down here.
