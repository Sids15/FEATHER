\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A model that is accurate the day you deploy it does not stay that way on its
own. The data keeps moving, and only some of that movement is harmless. In a
production system this is a routine operational headache, not some rare edge
case. Sculley et al.\ put undeclared data dependencies and shifting input
distributions on their list of the main sources of technical debt in machine
learning systems \cite{sculley2015debt}. Monitoring for this kind of
\emph{concept drift} is awkward for one plain reason: the labels that would
actually settle whether the model is still right show up late, or not at all
\cite{gama2014survey, lu2018learning}. So people fall back on label-free
proxies. One kind watches the input distribution and fires on any change at
all, which drowns the operator in alerts about shifts the model handles
without trouble \cite{rabanser2019failing}. A newer and more useful kind
watches the model's \emph{outputs}: confidence tracking
\cite{hendrycks2017baseline}, differences of confidences
\cite{guillory2021doc}, learned confidence thresholds \cite{garg2022atc},
prediction-uncertainty indices \cite{pudd2025}, and error-proxy models paired
with sequential testing \cite{amoukou2024sequential}.

Our whole starting point is one structural fact about that second kind.
Anything you compute from the model's outputs is, by definition, blind to any
change that leaves those outputs alone. That sounds like an empty statement
until you ask how much room ``leaves the outputs alone'' really covers. For a
softmax classifier the answer is: nearly all of activation space. The Fisher
information matrix of the log-likelihood with respect to the penultimate
activation $\act$ has rank at most $C-1$, so a ResNet-18 on CIFAR-10 has
outputs that respond to only a $9$-dimensional slice of a $512$-dimensional
feature space. The other $503$ directions push every logit up by the same
amount; the softmax undoes it; confidence and entropy never move. Drift the
world along those directions and the model has no way to respond, and no
monitor reading its outputs does either. If failure comes, it comes silently.

FEATHER (Fisher-Eigenvalue Adaptive Thresholding for Error-Robust drift
detection) watches that region head-on. Offline, a single pass over a labeled
held-out set produces the Fisher matrix in closed form; it is only
$d \times d$, so there is no need for a Kronecker or diagonal approximation.
Eigendecomposing it splits activation space into a sensitive part and a blind
part. Bootstrapping over clean reference batches then pins the alarm
thresholds to a chosen false-alarm rate. Online, all a batch costs is one
matrix--vector projection.

The paper contributes three things.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item{A two-line impossibility result
    (Proposition~\ref{prop:blind}): the blind subspace is exactly the set of
    softmax-invariant directions, so drift kept inside it defeats every
    output-based monitor by construction, not by bad luck.}
  \item{The FEATHER monitor itself: exact offline computation, millisecond
    online cost, three complementary statistics, and thresholds set to a
    chosen false-alarm budget.}
  \item{An evaluation on Rotated MNIST and CIFAR-10-C in which harmfulness is
    \emph{measured} from held-out labels instead of assumed from corruption
    names, with a comparison against output-based baselines and a covariance
    ablation under held-out calibration, a deep-feature experiment that
    exposes the blind spot on a trained ResNet-18, and a frank account of
    where standard corruption benchmarks stop telling you anything.}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Drift Detection as Change Detection}

Sequential change detection has a long history. Page's CUSUM chart goes back
to 1954 \cite{page1954cusum}, and the stream-mining literature carried it
into learning systems as hypothesis tests over a running stream. DDM watches
the classifier's error rate for a significant jump \cite{gama2004learning},
EDDM adapts that to gradual drift by tracking the gap between errors
\cite{baena2006eddm}, ADWIN keeps adaptive sub-windows of a scalar statistic
and compares them \cite{bifet2007adwin}, and KSWIN runs a Kolmogorov--Smirnov
test over sliding windows \cite{raab2020kswin}; the River library
\cite{montiel2021river} ships mature versions of all of them. These
error-rate detectors have the right idea but the wrong input: they need
labels, and labels are exactly what a stream does not give you. The surveys
\cite{gama2014survey, lu2018learning} treat that label-scarcity problem as
the central, still-open one.

The distribution-level alternatives sidestep labels by asking, instead,
whether the inputs have changed. They reach for kernel two-sample statistics
such as MMD \cite{gretton2012kernel} or for classifier-based tests; the
covariate/label/concept taxonomy is set out in \cite{quinonero2009dataset},
and label-shift estimation in \cite{lipton2018label}. By construction these
tests say nothing about performance. Rabanser et al.\
\cite{rabanser2019failing} made the consequence concrete: how strongly a
shift is detected and how much damage it does line up poorly, and
characterizing \emph{harmful} shift was left as an open problem. That is the
gap this paper goes after.

\subsection{Confidence, Uncertainty, and Label-Free Accuracy Estimation}

A newer line goes straight at the operational question: how well is the model
doing right now, with no labels to check against? The
maximum-softmax-probability baseline \cite{hendrycks2017baseline} kicked off
the habit of reading that answer off the model's own confidence, and later
work sharpened the readout: DoC regresses accuracy on differences of
confidences \cite{guillory2021doc}, ATC learns a confidence threshold whose
exceedance rate estimates target accuracy \cite{garg2022atc}, and PUDD picks
up drift early from prediction uncertainty \cite{pudd2025}. Two well-known
facts get in the way, though: modern networks are miscalibrated in a
systematic way \cite{guo2017calibration}, and the calibration slips further
under exactly the dataset shift you are trying to detect
\cite{ovadia2019trust}. Closest to what we do, Amoukou et al.\
\cite{amoukou2024sequential} formalize sequential harmful-shift detection
without labels using an error proxy and a sequential test, and we take our
problem framing from them.

But every method in that paragraph draws its signal from the model's outputs,
or from a model trained to mimic them, and so it inherits the blind spot of
Proposition~\ref{prop:blind}. We read them as complements rather than rivals:
they handle the drift the model can see, and FEATHER handles the drift it
cannot.

\subsection{Feature-Space and Out-of-Distribution Detection}

Working in the penultimate activation space is routine in out-of-distribution
detection. The Mahalanobis method \cite{lee2018mahalanobis} scores each
sample against class-conditional Gaussians fit to the features, and Rabanser
et al.'s strongest shift detector (BBSD) likewise runs its test on the
model's representations \cite{rabanser2019failing}. There are also label-free
drift detectors that read activations straight, never routing through the
outputs: Komorniczak and Ksieniewicz \cite{komorniczak2024parallel} pull
drift signals out of a network's parallel activation patterns with no labels
at all, even offline. What none of these has is a way to say \emph{which}
feature directions the task actually cares about; they weight feature space
by variance, by density, or by whatever structure the architecture imposes,
never by the effect on the model's decisions. That task-supervised Fisher
weighting is what sets FEATHER apart from this line. The benchmarks in this
area, the common-corruptions suite \cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking} we use
among them, and the in-the-wild shifts of WILDS \cite{koh2021wilds}, likewise
make no distinction between shift the model shrugs off and shift that hurts
it, a point we come back to in Sect.~\ref{sec:saturation}.

\subsection{Fisher Geometry of Neural Networks}

The Fisher matrix mostly reaches deep learning through optimization and
continual learning: natural gradient \cite{amari1998natural}, its KFAC
approximation \cite{martens2015kfac}, and Fisher-weighted regularization
against forgetting \cite{kirkpatrick2017overcoming}. Karakida et al.\
\cite{karakida2019universal} found that deep-network Fisher spectra pile up in
a few large eigenvalues over a near-zero bulk, and that concentration is the
structure we lean on. In our activation-space version, though, the split is
exact rather than statistical, since it drops out of an algebraic rank bound
(Sect.~\ref{sec:blind}). Two other papers push related geometry toward
monitoring. Zhang et al.\ \cite{zhang2023score} follow the Fisher \emph{score
vector} with multivariate control charts, but the score is a different object,
a per-sample gradient in parameter space that needs labels or a stand-in for
them, and their charts hand back a detection signal, not a criterion for harm.
Zero-Direction Probing \cite{pandey2025zdp} looks at null directions of
activations for representational drift in language models, without any
task-supervised Fisher matrix and without a harmful/benign split. FEATHER
lands in the gap between \cite{amoukou2024sequential}, who have the problem
but not the geometry, and \cite{pandey2025zdp}, who have the geometry but not
the problem. Table~\ref{tab:positioning} lays out the landscape.

\section{Problem Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

Let $f(x) = \softmax(W\act(x) + b)$ be a deployed, frozen $C$-class
classifier with penultimate activation $\act(x) \in \R^d$,
$W \in \R^{C \times d}$, $b \in \R^C$. Unlabeled batches $B_t$ come in off a
stream. Just once, offline, we are handed a labeled held-out set
$\{(\act_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$. The job is to raise an alarm when the stream
has drifted in a way that would plausibly cost accuracy, and to stay quiet
when the drift is something the model can absorb. Above all, it has to manage
this in the regime where the model's own outputs tell you nothing.

The single piece of structure the rest of the paper leans on is elementary.
For any $c \in \R$,
\begin{equation}
  \softmax(z + c\,\mathbf{1}) = \softmax(z),
  \label{eq:invariance}
\end{equation}
the softmax does not notice a constant added to every logit. So any
activation perturbation $u$ with $Wu \propto \mathbf{1}$ is invisible in the
outputs, one sample at a time. Figure~\ref{fig:geometry} draws the picture.

\section{The FEATHER Monitor}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{The Activation-Space Fisher Matrix and Its Blind Subspace}
\label{sec:blind}

We take as monitoring object the empirical Fisher information of the
log-likelihood \emph{with respect to the activation},
\begin{equation}
  \F \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} g_i g_i^\top,
  \qquad
  g_i \;=\; \nabla_{\act}\log p(y_i \mid \act_i)
        \;=\; W^\top\!\big(e_{y_i} - p_i\big),
  \label{eq:fisher}
\end{equation}
with $p_i = \softmax(W\act_i + b)$ and $e_y$ the one-hot vector. Every $g_i$
lies in $W^\top\!\mathcal{H}$, where
$\mathcal{H} = \{v \in \R^C : \mathbf{1}^\top v = 0\}$ (the residual
$e_y - p$ sums to zero), so
\begin{equation}
  \rank \F \;\le\; \min\{\rank W,\; C-1\} \;\ll\; d .
  \label{eq:rank}
\end{equation}
$\F$ is $d \times d$ and computed exactly in one pass; the eigendecomposition
takes milliseconds. Writing $\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_d$ for its
eigenvalues with orthonormal eigenvectors $u_1,\dots,u_d$, we call
$\mathcal{S} = \spn\{u_j : \lambda_j > \tau\lambda_1\}$ the \emph{sensitive}
subspace and $\mathcal{N} = \mathcal{S}^\perp$ the \emph{blind} subspace. The
rank gap makes the split insensitive to $\tau$: in our ResNet-18 experiments
$\lambda_9/\lambda_1 \approx 5 \times 10^{-2}$ while
$\lambda_{10}/\lambda_1 \approx 10^{-16}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:spectrum}), so any
tolerance within fourteen orders of magnitude gives the same answer.

One point of terminology is worth pausing on. Since each $g_i$ uses the
\emph{observed} label $y_i$, our $\F$ is the empirical, label-conditioned
Fisher, not the Fisher information proper, which averages over the model's own
predictive distribution instead; the two can diverge in optimization
\cite{kunstner2019empirical}. For us the difference is one of convenience, not
substance: the true Fisher's per-sample covariance $\diag(p) - pp^\top$ also
has rows that sum to zero, so it lands in $W^\top\!\mathcal{H}$ too. The rank
bound (\ref{eq:rank}), and with it the blind subspace of
Proposition~\ref{prop:blind}, comes out the same for either object. We go with
the empirical one because the labels are there offline and it reads the
task-relevant gradients off directly. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the full
pipeline.

\subsection{Online Statistics}
\label{sec:stats}

Let $\mu_{\mathrm{ref}}$ be the reference activation mean and $B_\mathcal{N}$
an orthonormal basis of $\mathcal{N}$. For a batch with mean $\mu_t$ and
shift $\Delta\mu_t = \mu_t - \mu_{\mathrm{ref}}$ we keep three numbers. The
direction ratio
\begin{equation}
  s_t = \frac{\lVert B_\mathcal{N}^\top \Delta\mu_t\rVert}
             {\lVert \Delta\mu_t\rVert}
\end{equation}
asks which way the drift points; close to one means almost all of the
movement sits inside the blind subspace. The magnitude
$m_t = \lVert B_\mathcal{N}^\top \Delta\mu_t\rVert$ asks whether it is large
enough to care about. By itself $s_t$ is scale-free and would happily flag
noise that just happens to point the wrong way. The energy
\begin{equation}
  v_t = \frac{1}{|B_t|}\sum_{x \in B_t}
        \big\lVert B_\mathcal{N}^\top(\act(x) - \mu_{\mathrm{ref}})\big\rVert^2
\end{equation}
picks up drift that leaks into $\mathcal{N}$ without shifting the mean.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:feather} assembles the parts. Online, the whole cost is a
single $d \times k$ projection per batch, with $k = \dim\mathcal{N}$.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{FEATHER}
\label{alg:feather}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Statex \textbf{Offline} (once, labeled reference set):
\State compute $g_i = W^\top(e_{y_i} - p_i)$ and
       $\F = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i g_i g_i^\top$
\State eigendecompose $\F$; split at the rank gap into
       $\mathcal{S} \oplus \mathcal{N}$; store basis $B_\mathcal{N}$,
       reference mean $\mu_{\mathrm{ref}}$
\State bootstrap clean reference batches; set thresholds
       $\theta_m, \theta_v$ at the $(1-\alpha)$ quantile of $m, v$
\Statex \textbf{Online} (per unlabeled batch $B_t$):
\State $\Delta\mu_t \gets \mathrm{mean}_{x \in B_t}\,\act(x) - \mu_{\mathrm{ref}}$
\State compute $s_t, m_t, v_t$ from $B_\mathcal{N}^\top$ projections
\State \textbf{alarm} if $m_t > \theta_m$ or $v_t > \theta_v$;
       report $s_t$ as the blind fraction of the shift
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Calibration}

The thresholds for $m_t$ and $v_t$ are quantiles of their bootstrap
distribution over clean reference batches, which is what makes the stationary
false-alarm rate a number you choose rather than a constant you tune.
Throughout, we use 500 bootstrap batches and the 99th percentile. The
reference data has to do two jobs that must never see the same samples: a
\emph{geometry} split fits the Fisher and covariance bases, and a disjoint
\emph{calibration} split, a deterministic 50/50 partition, supplies
$\mu_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and the bootstrap thresholds. Calibrate on the fitting
split and the fit bleeds into the thresholds and flatters them;
Sect.~\ref{sec:calibration} measures how much.

\section{Theory}
\label{sec:theory}

\begin{assumption}
\label{ass:span}
The reference gradients $\{g_i\}$ span $W^\top\!\mathcal{H}$. This holds
almost surely once $C$ generic samples are seen; empirically we observe the
full rank $C-1$ in every run.
\end{assumption}

The full rank $C-1$ requires $\rank W \ge C-1$, which every model here
satisfies. In a rank-deficient head ($\rank W < C-1$, e.g.\ a bottlenecked
or weight-tied final layer), $\dim W^\top\!\mathcal{H} \le \rank W$: the
sensitive subspace only shrinks and the blind subspace only grows.
Proposition~\ref{prop:blind}'s characterization of $\mathcal{N}$ is
unchanged; only the dimension count $d - C + 1$ becomes
$d - \dim W^\top\!\mathcal{H} \ge d - \rank W$.

Before the formal statement it helps to picture $\mathcal{N}$ in the smallest
case. Take two classes ($C = 2$) and a two-dimensional feature space
($d = 2$), with weight vectors $w_1, w_2 \in \R^2$, the rows of $W$. The
classifier's decision rides on the single logit difference
$(w_1 - w_2)^\top\act + (b_1 - b_2)$, so the one direction the outputs
actually track is $w_1 - w_2$, the normal to the decision boundary, and
everything orthogonal to it is blind. Put $w_1 = (1,0)$ and $w_2 = (0,1)$: the
sensitive direction is $w_1 - w_2 \propto (1,-1)$ and the blind direction is
$(1,1)$. Shifting $\act$ along $(1,1)$ adds the same amount to both logits,
since $W(1,1)^\top = (1,1)^\top = \mathbf{1}$, and the softmax cancels it. You
can move the data as far as you like along $(1,1)$ and the confidence, the
entropy, and the predicted label all sit exactly where they were.
Proposition~\ref{prop:blind} is this picture at full dimension: the outputs
see the $(C-1)$-dimensional span of logit differences, and the remaining
$d - C + 1$ directions make up the blind subspace.

\begin{proposition}[Detector blindness]
\label{prop:blind}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:span},
\[
  \mathcal{N} \;=\; \{\, u \in \R^d \;:\; Wu \in \spn\{\mathbf{1}\} \,\},
\]
the set of softmax-invariant directions. Any drift that only translates
activations within $\mathcal{N}$ leaves the model's outputs pointwise
unchanged, and with them every monitoring statistic computed from outputs:
confidence, entropy, prediction histograms, DoC, ATC, uncertainty indices,
and error proxies trained on outputs.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\mathcal{S} = \spn\{g_i\} = W^\top\!\mathcal{H}$ by the assumption, so
$u \in \mathcal{N}$ iff $(Wu)^\top v = 0$ for all $v \in \mathcal{H}$, i.e.\
iff $Wu \in \mathcal{H}^\perp = \spn\{\mathbf{1}\}$. For such $u$,
$W(\act + tu) + b = (W\act + b) + tc\,\mathbf{1}$ for some $c$, and by
(\ref{eq:invariance}) the softmax is unchanged. Outputs are unchanged input
by input, hence so is any functional of them. \qed
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Calibrated response in the sensitive subspace]
\label{prop:bounded}
For any perturbation $\Delta \in \R^d$ and class $y$,
$\big|\log p(y \mid \act + \Delta) - \log p(y \mid \act)\big|
\le 2\lVert W\Delta \rVert_\infty$. The response to sensitive-subspace drift
is bounded and continuous in the shift; the response to blind-subspace drift
is exactly zero.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\log p(y \mid \act) = z_y - \mathrm{logsumexp}(z)$ with $z = W\act + b$. The
linear term moves by at most $\lVert W\Delta\rVert_\infty$, and logsumexp is
$1$-Lipschitz in the $\ell_\infty$ norm. The blind case is
Proposition~\ref{prop:blind}. \qed
\end{proof}

A word on what this does and does not claim.
Proposition~\ref{prop:blind} is an impossibility result about output-based
monitoring; it is not a promise that blind drift is harmful. First-order
geometry cannot manufacture errors on its own. What makes the blind region
dangerous is that the model has no way to respond there, so if the label
function or the upstream feature extractor moves along one of those
directions, the output stream stays silent about it. Whether blind-subspace
movement really does travel with damage in deep networks is an empirical
matter, and Sect.~\ref{sec:experiments} measures it instead of assuming it.
The subspace belongs to the frozen head; when the model is retrained it is
recomputed exactly, in seconds, and we hold it fixed between retrainings
rather than tracking it online (streaming decompositions do exist
\cite{halko2011structure}, but we did not need one).

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}

\runinhead{Models and Training.}
A small CNN (two convolutional blocks, $d = 128$) trained on MNIST with Adam
(learning rate $10^{-3}$, 5 epochs) reaches $98.96 \pm 0.05\%$ test accuracy,
and a CIFAR-adapted ResNet-18 ($3{\times}3$ stem, no max-pool, $d = 512$,
11.2M parameters) trained on CIFAR-10 with SGD (learning rate 0.1, cosine
schedule, 50 epochs, standard crop-and-flip augmentation) reaches
$94.14 \pm 0.20\%$. We run five seeds per model; a seed fixes the
initialization, the data order, and the augmentation, and every run resumes
from checkpoints with the full RNG state.

\runinhead{Drift Streams.}
The MNIST test set rotated from $0^\circ$ to $90^\circ$ in $15^\circ$ steps,
and CIFAR-10-C \cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking} (19 corruptions $\times$ 5
severities, plus the clean test set), both streamed in batches of 500. An
\emph{episode} is a single corruption-severity pair, or a single angle, for
one seed: $96 \times 5 = 480$ CIFAR episodes and $7 \times 5 = 35$ MNIST
episodes, which is 1{,}920 monitored batches per CIFAR seed.

\runinhead{Ground Truth.}
We tag episodes by their \emph{measured} accuracy drop against the clean
episode, benign below 2 points, harmful above 10, gray in between, rather than
by the name of the corruption. Under that rule the CIFAR episodes come out 62
benign, 127 gray, 291 harmful. The split earns its keep: fog at severity 1 is
benign while fog at severity 5 costs more than 30 points, so lumping both
under ``fog'' would just average them into noise.

\runinhead{Detectors.}
FEATHER; a covariance ablation of FEATHER (the same pipeline with $\F$
swapped for the activation covariance at the same blind dimension, which
isolates the Fisher weighting); and four output-based baselines: mean
max-softmax confidence \cite{hendrycks2017baseline}, mean prediction entropy,
an ATC-style accuracy estimate \cite{garg2022atc}, and a logistic error proxy
after \cite{amoukou2024sequential} fit on the labeled reference. Each baseline
collapses a batch to one scalar and alarms on a bootstrap quantile of it, the
same way FEATHER calibrates, so the false-alarm comparison happens at a
matched budget. The episode scores used in the saturation analysis are
threshold-free changes measured against the clean episode.

\runinhead{Hardware and Reproducibility.}
A single workstation GPU (RTX 4500 Ada, 24 GB). One ResNet-18 seed takes
376 s to train; the MNIST model takes 23 s. Every figure and table in this
section is regenerated from the logged runs by a script in the repository.

\subsection{A Linear Testbed Where Every Answer Is Known}

Ahead of the deep networks, we check the method on two-dimensional
two-Gaussian streams whose Bayes boundary is known in closed form, so the
sensitive and blind directions have exact analytical values to compare
against. The implementation recovers the blind eigenvector to
$|\cos| > 1 - 10^{-6}$. Drift four standard deviations along the blind
direction and the softmax outputs move by less than $10^{-10}$ per sample
(Proposition~\ref{prop:blind}, now numerically), yet the calibrated monitor
flags all 15 post-onset batches with $s_t > 0.95$. Drift along the sensitive
direction and the same monitor stays on its noise floor ($\bar{s}_t < 0.2$,
no alarms). Over 200 clean batches the stationary alarm rate holds inside the
calibrated budget. All 38 unit and integration tests, these among them,
finish in under a second on CPU.

\subsection{The Predicted Rank Structure Appears Exactly}

Equation (\ref{eq:rank}) caps the Fisher rank at $C - 1 = 9$.
Figure~\ref{fig:spectrum} plots the measured spectra: in all ten runs,
exactly nine eigenvalues clear machine precision. On a representative
CIFAR-10 seed, $\lambda_9 = 9.1 \times 10^{-5}$ while
$\lambda_{10} = 5 \times 10^{-19}$, a fourteen-order drop, which leaves a
503-dimensional blind subspace inside a 512-dimensional feature space ($119$
of $128$ for the MNIST CNN). So the claim from the introduction, that
output-based monitors watch a 9-dimensional slice of a 512-dimensional space,
is meant literally and not as a figure of speech. It also fixes the blind
dimension with no tuning knob: drop the threshold anywhere across a
fourteen-decade window and you get the same subspace.

\subsection{Where the Benchmark Stops Discriminating}
\label{sec:saturation}

At the episode level this benchmark, it turns out, does not separate the
detectors at all: FEATHER, the covariance ablation, and the confidence and
entropy baselines all pull harmful from benign at AUROC $1.0$, on both
benchmarks and on every seed (Table~\ref{tab:auroc}). We report the
saturation rather than bury it, and take the honest lesson from it: standard
corruption benchmarks produce \emph{output-visible} drift, the kind where
output-based monitors do fine and FEATHER only matches them. That squares
with \cite{rabanser2019failing}, who saw representation-level and
output-level tests alike fire on synthetic corruptions; the benchmarks were,
after all, built to be seen. What still tells the monitors apart once
detection saturates is how they behave under calibration: how often each one
alarms on drift that does no harm. That is the next question.

\subsection{Deep-Feature Silent Drift}
\label{sec:blinddeep}

The benchmarks so far are output-visible by construction, so none of them can
probe the one regime where Proposition~\ref{prop:blind} hands FEATHER a
monopoly: drift the outputs simply \emph{cannot} express. To get there we have
to manufacture drift that lives inside the blind subspace, and we do it
directly on the trained ResNet-18, reusing its saved penultimate activations
and blind basis, with no retraining. Onto a clean batch of activations we add
a shift $\alpha u$ of growing size along a unit direction $u$, trying two
choices of $u$: one drawn from the empirically identified blind subspace
$\mathcal{N}$, and one from its sensitive complement $\mathcal{S}$. For each we
record three things: the largest change the shift forces in any softmax
output, which is everything an output-based monitor could ever hope to see;
FEATHER's alarm rate; and, so that ``harmful'' stays measured rather than
assumed, the accuracy under a synthetic label rule.

That label rule is what lets a shift the model cannot see still count as real
damage. We let the ground-truth label depend on the sample's coordinate along
the blind direction $u$, the very coordinate the frozen head discards, and we
flip the label once a shifted sample's coordinate moves past its clean range.
The correct answer therefore changes along $u$ while every logit, and with it
every output statistic, stays frozen. This is the silent failure from the
introduction made concrete: the world has moved somewhere the model is
structurally unable to follow, the outputs report business as usual, and only
a monitor watching $\mathcal{N}$ has any way to notice. The
sensitive-direction run is the mirror-image control, where the same
construction should move the outputs, wake the output monitors, and leave
FEATHER quiet.

The contrast could not be sharper (Table~\ref{tab:blinddeep}, five seeds,
$\alpha=0.3$). A blind-subspace shift big enough to set FEATHER off on every
batch moves the most-changed softmax probability by under $2\times10^{-10}$
and mean confidence and entropy by under $10^{-12}$. That invisibility is
numerical, not a matter of approximation, exactly as the algebra says it
should be on a real network. The very same shift pushes the synthetic accuracy
from $99\%$ down to $7\%$: harmful drift that no confidence-, entropy-, or
proxy-based monitor can react to, and that FEATHER flags on $100\%$ of batches.
The sensitive-subspace control flips every cell: the outputs move (the top
probability shifts by $0.06$ at the same $\alpha$), an output monitor sees it,
and FEATHER, watching only $\mathcal{N}$, sits correctly at its $2\%$ floor.
Figure~\ref{fig:blinddeep} follows both effects as $\alpha$ grows: FEATHER's
alarm rate climbs through its calibrated threshold near $\alpha\approx0.1$ and
the synthetic error rises with it, while the output change never lifts off the
$10^{-10}$ floor. This is a \emph{deep-feature} confirmation of the blind spot.
Because the drift is injected in activation space and the harm comes from a
synthetic rule, it establishes output-invisibility and FEATHER detection on a
real network without yet claiming a pixel-space, real-label attack
(Sect.~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Where the False Alarms Land}

Since the benchmarks saturate on detection, the honest question becomes
calibration: with every monitor held to the same nominal budget, how often
does each one cry wolf on drift that leaves accuracy alone?
Table~\ref{tab:alarms} sets all six against the same CIFAR-10-C episodes. The
four output baselines (ATC, confidence drop, entropy rise, and an error proxy
after \cite{amoukou2024sequential}) are calibrated on a held-out split of the
clean deployment stream rather than on the fitting reference; without that
they alarm on every clean test batch, thanks to the train-to-test confidence
gap (Sect.~\ref{sec:calibration}). FEATHER and the covariance ablation keep
their reference-split thresholds.

On these benchmarks the output baselines take the false-alarm comparison,
which is just what Proposition~\ref{prop:blind} would predict: the drift here
is output-visible, so confidence and entropy track it and go quiet when
accuracy holds ($6$--$9\%$ benign alarms). FEATHER fires more often
($47.6 \pm 6.9\%$), because a benign corruption can still move the blind
subspace even while it spares accuracy. Against its own covariance ablation,
though, FEATHER wins clearly: the ablation fires on $82.9 \pm 6.9\%$ of benign
batches, so swapping Fisher weighting for raw variance almost doubles the
false alarms, a gap of $35.3 \pm 3.6$ points that stays positive on every seed
(an exact paired sign-flip test gives $p = 0.0625$, the smallest this design
can produce). A benign corruption, or even plain clean resampling, excites
low-variance directions easily, which is where the ablation's $28\%$ clean
rate comes from. Low-Fisher directions are far harder to excite. So the honest
reading is not that FEATHER beats the output family on corruption benchmarks.
It does not, and it should not, because these benchmarks never leave the
region the outputs can see. The order flips once they do. In the deep-feature
silent-drift experiment (Sect.~\ref{sec:blinddeep},
Table~\ref{tab:blinddeep}) the four output baselines register nothing at all,
since their inputs do not budge to nine decimal places, and FEATHER is the
only monitor that alarms. FEATHER is a complement to output-based monitoring,
not a replacement: quiet where the outputs already do the job, and the only
witness left where they go blind.

\subsection{A Calibration Finding}
\label{sec:calibration}

An earlier version of this pipeline set the bootstrap thresholds on the very
same reference split that fits the Fisher matrix, and it turned out too
optimistic: the fit leaks into the thresholds, and even a mild clean shift
slipped past them. So every number here uses the held-out protocol of
Sect.~\ref{sec:method}: the bases come from the geometry split and the
thresholds from a disjoint calibration split. That correction brings the
ResNet-18's clean-stream false alarms back onto the nominal budget
(Table~\ref{tab:alarms}), which pins the covariance ablation's $28\%$
clean-stream rate on the geometry it monitors rather than on leaked
thresholds. One honest residual is left: on the small MNIST CNN, even
held-out thresholds alarm on the \emph{unrotated} test set, because that
model's train-to-test activation shift carries a bigger blind-subspace
component than within-train resampling would suggest. The episode-level
conclusions are untouched, since every score is taken relative to the clean
episode, but in deployment the thresholds ought to be calibrated on reference
data pulled from the deployment stream, not from training data alone.

We checked this head-on, and symmetrically. Recalibrating FEATHER's reference
mean and bootstrap thresholds on a held-out split of the clean \emph{test}
stream (the protocol the output baselines already follow, which puts the
comparison at matched calibration) keeps its clean-stream false-alarm rate
low on both benchmarks: $0.10 \pm 0.06$ on CIFAR-10-C and $0.08 \pm 0.04$ on
the MNIST CNN across the five seeds, against a covariance ablation at $0.02$
and $0.10$. So the false-alarm comparison we report does not lean on handing
FEATHER a training-only reference the baselines are refused. The separation
the paper rests on holds up under this calibration too: re-scoring every
CIFAR-10-C episode against the clean-test reference, the blind subspace still
alarms on fewer benign shifts than the covariance ablation ($0.59 \pm 0.05$
versus $0.67 \pm 0.05$) while both catch every harmful one ($1.00 \pm 0.00$),
so FEATHER's benign--harmful margin is a fact about the geometry it monitors,
not about a lucky reference split. The MNIST number is the sharper one: it
drops from an above-budget training-calibrated value back to budget as soon as
the reference is drawn from the deployment stream, which puts its earlier
clean alarms down to the model's train-to-test activation gap and not to
FEATHER's geometry. It echoes the familiar finding that uncertainty tooling
gets worse right where you need it most \cite{ovadia2019trust}.

\subsection{Cost}

For the ResNet-18, the one-time offline fit (activation extraction over
50{,}000 reference images, the exact Fisher matrix, the eigendecomposition,
and 500 bootstrap batches) takes 11.1 s on the workstation GPU. Online,
monitoring adds 48 ms per 500-image batch on top of the forward pass, and the
projection itself, a $512 \times 503$ matrix--vector product, is microseconds
of that. Monitoring costs strictly less than the inference it sits on, which
matters, because a monitor that fights the model for GPU time is a monitor
that gets turned off. The offline cost is $O(nd^2)$ for building the Fisher
plus $O(d^3)$ for the eigendecomposition, negligible at $d = 512$ and still a
one-time cost of seconds at the $d \approx 4096$ of large vision backbones.
Past that, the rank structure plays into randomized eigensolvers
\cite{halko2011structure}, since only the top $C-1$ eigenvectors ever matter
for defining $\mathcal{N}$. The online projection stays a single
matrix--vector product at any $d$.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

It is worth being plain about what the two benchmark regimes establish,
because they point in opposite directions and both readings matter. On the
public corruption suites FEATHER does not beat the output-based monitors on
false alarms, and Proposition~\ref{prop:blind} is exactly why: that drift is
output-visible, so confidence and entropy have real signal to work with and
they use it well. The regime where FEATHER is the only monitor left standing
is the silent one of Sect.~\ref{sec:blinddeep}, which those benchmarks never
enter. So the practical picture is not FEATHER \emph{versus} the output
family; it is the two of them side by side. An operator can run a cheap
confidence- or entropy-based monitor for the visible failures and FEATHER for
the blind ones, and because the two alarm on disjoint parts of drift space,
neither one makes the other redundant.

The comparison we hold most tightly fixed is the one against the covariance
ablation, and it isolates the single design choice we most want to defend:
weighting activation directions by the task, through the Fisher matrix,
instead of by their variance. Same pipeline, same blind dimension, same
calibration; only the weighting changes, and trading Fisher for covariance
nearly doubles the benign false alarms (Table~\ref{tab:alarms}). The reason is
geometric. A benign corruption, or even ordinary resampling, stirs the
low-variance directions a covariance monitor treats as informative, while the
low-Fisher directions FEATHER discards are precisely the ones the model's
decisions never depend on. Task-aware weighting is not a tuning detail here;
it is what keeps the monitor quiet on the drift the model can absorb.

The saturation result is a finding in its own right. Every detector we tried,
ours included, pulls harmful from benign at ceiling on both suites
(Table~\ref{tab:auroc}), which means these benchmarks cannot rank label-free
monitors on the question deployment actually asks. Two habits would help, and
we follow both: score an episode's harm from held-out labels rather than read
it off a corruption name, and judge a monitor by its false-alarm discipline on
benign drift, not by detection power alone. Better still would be benchmarks
that build in the silent regime on purpose, realistic shift that leaves the
outputs untouched, since that is where label-free monitoring is currently
blind and where the real differences between methods have to show up.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The blind subspace is exact for the softmax head, but it only captures deeper
feature drift as far as the penultimate layer summarizes it. A multi-layer
version through a Kronecker-factored parameter-space Fisher
\cite{martens2015kfac} is the natural extension. Proposition~\ref{prop:blind}
says what output-based monitors cannot see; it does not say blind drift has to
hurt. Pure concept shift that leaves the activation marginals alone is
invisible to every label-free method too, ours among them. Our benchmarks,
like most public corruption suites, generate output-visible drift, so they
test FEATHER's tracking and false-alarm behavior but not the silent regime
where Proposition~\ref{prop:blind} gives it a monopoly.
Section~\ref{sec:blinddeep} does reach that regime on the trained network, but
only semi-synthetically, with the drift injected in activation space and the
harm set by a synthetic label rule. Closing the gap to a pixel-space
perturbation whose feature displacement lands in $\mathcal{N}$ under unchanged
real labels, perhaps drawn from the wild shifts cataloged in WILDS
\cite{koh2021wilds} rather than synthetic corruptions, is, we think, the
follow-up worth most. Held-out calibration only holds the clean false-alarm
rate at budget when the reference data matches the deployment stream; where
they differ, as with the MNIST CNN's train-to-test activation shift, the
thresholds have to be recalibrated on deployment-matched data
(Sect.~\ref{sec:calibration}). Two more extensions fall out of the design and
we leave them for later. One is a cascade that runs a cheap classical change
detector up front and only calls FEATHER to sort a flagged shift into
output-visible or blind, buying lower average cost for a little latency. The
other, since the blind subspace belongs to the frozen head, is an
intermediate-layer version through a Kronecker-factored Fisher
\cite{martens2015kfac}, for drift that is already gone before the penultimate
features.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Label-free monitoring has settled on reading the model's outputs, and the
geometry of a softmax head makes the price of that choice exact: the outputs
are a $(C-1)$-dimensional window onto a $d$-dimensional representation, and
everything orthogonal to the window is invisible. FEATHER computes the
invisible part exactly and watches it for the cost of a projection. In our
experiments it follows measured damage while cutting its own covariance
ablation's false alarms in half. It is not a replacement for confidence-based
tools. It complements them, covering the one region they provably cannot
reach.
